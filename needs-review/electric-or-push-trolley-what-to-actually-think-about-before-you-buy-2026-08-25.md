Choosing between an electric or push trolley is one of those decisions that looks simple until you start reading about it, at which point you end up in a rabbit hole of lithium batteries, follow modes, and GPS integrations. This post tries to cut through that and give you a straightforward checklist based on how most UK club golfers actually play.

## Start With Your Course, Not the Spec Sheet

The single most useful question to ask before you buy is: what is the terrain actually like where you play?

Electric trolleys earn their cost most clearly on hilly or undulating courses. If your home track has a couple of long climbs or awkward cambers, a motorised trolley is doing real work for you. It takes the strain off your shoulders on the way up and keeps your bag under control on the way down. On genuinely challenging terrain, the premium over a push trolley starts to feel reasonable pretty quickly.

On flat parkland, the benefit is real but more modest. You are still not carrying, which matters, but the physical effort of pushing a well-built three-wheel push trolley on a flat course is not significant. If your regular courses are mostly level, that should factor into your thinking.

## The Practical Checklist

Before you decide, work through these points honestly.

**How often do you play?**
If you are out three or more times a week, an electric trolley makes more sense. The convenience compounds over many rounds. If you play once a week or less, a push trolley is perfectly adequate and considerably cheaper to buy and run.

**What is your car boot situation?**
Electric trolleys are heavier and bulkier when folded than push trolleys. Some models fold down tidily; others are genuinely awkward. If you drive a smaller car or share it, this matters more than the brochures tend to admit. Push trolleys are lighter, fold smaller, and generally take up less space.

**Do you want the exercise element?**
Some golfers actively want to push. If walking the course with some physical effort is part of why you enjoy the game, there is no shame in a push trolley. It is not a step backwards.

**Is there a back or shoulder consideration?**
If pushing a trolley on undulating ground is uncomfortable, an electric trolley is a sensible practical choice. This is generic guidance rather than medical advice, but it is worth being honest with yourself about.

## Push Trolleys: What You Get

Push trolleys start from around £100 and there are solid options from brands including Big Max, Motocaddy, PowaKaddy, Sun Mountain, and Benross, among others. The Benross Pro Compact has been noted as a popular and well-engineered option at under £150, with a steel frame, ergonomic handles, and an umbrella holder included.

At this end of the market, the differences between models are mostly about build quality, folding mechanism, and stability on slopes. A three-wheel design tends to be more stable than two-wheel on uneven ground. There is no battery to charge, no motor to maintain, and no question of whether it will last a full 18.

## Electric Trolleys: What You Get (and What You Pay)

Electric trolleys are the dominant format in the UK now. Entry-level models from brands like Motocaddy and PowaKaddy start at roughly £429 to £499. At the top end, GPS flagship models (such as the PowaKaddy CT12 GPS) can run to £1,200 or more.

The PowaKaddy FX3 is widely described as the best-selling electric trolley in the UK, with a twist-dial speed control and a straightforward display. The Motocaddy SE sits at the accessible end and comes with a choice of lead-acid or lithium battery.

### Lithium vs Lead-Acid Battery

This is worth understanding before you buy.

Lead-acid batteries are cheaper and bring the entry price down, but they are heavier. They also typically need to be removed from the trolley for charging, which is an extra step every time.

Lithium batteries cost more but are lighter, and on better models they do not need to be removed for charging. For a UK golfer storing a trolley in a garage and plugging it in after every round, that is a genuine convenience difference over a season.

### Advanced Features in 2026

Higher-end electric trolleys now come with built-in GPS (some with up to 40,000 preloaded courses), remote control, and follow modes where the trolley tracks you autonomously. The Stewart Golf Q Follow is one example of a follow-mode trolley.

One thing worth noting about follow mode: it requires the remote to be charged and working at all times. That is another piece of kit to keep maintained, and it is worth factoring in if you are someone who would find that an irritation rather than a novelty.

## Maintenance Notes

Electric trolleys need more attention than push trolleys. Clean the wheels after every round (mud and sand work into the motor mechanism over time) and keep the battery charged according to the manufacturer's guidance. Leaving a lithium battery fully discharged for extended periods shortens its life.

Push trolleys are low maintenance by comparison. Rinse the wheels, check the folding mechanism periodically, and that is about it.

## The Honest Summary

Push trolleys make most sense if you play on flat courses, play fewer than two or three times a week, have a smaller car, or want to keep costs down without compromising on the round itself.

Electric trolleys genuinely earn their cost on hilly or undulating courses and for golfers playing regularly. If you go electric, lithium battery models are the more convenient choice for how most people actually store and charge them at home. The GPS and follow-mode features are real, but they are extras rather than reasons to buy.

Work out what your course demands, be honest about how often you play, and check your boot size before you decide.
