## The Case for Staying Longer

Royal County Down pulls golfers in from all over the world, and it deserves every bit of the reputation. But flying to Belfast, driving to Newcastle, playing one round on a Monday morning, and heading straight back to the airport is a bit like visiting Rome to see the Colosseum and nothing else. County Down has a genuine golf coast, and treating it as a proper circuit over three or four days makes far more sense than a single-course pilgrimage.

This guide covers four courses across the county: the unmissable Championship Links at Royal County Down, the cliff-top links at Ardglass, the James Braid layout at Kirkistown Castle on the Ards Peninsula, and the parkland at Warrenpoint looking out over Carlingford Lough. Between them, they give you links, parkland, drama, history, and a range of green fees that won't require a second mortgage for every round.

## Royal County Down, Newcastle

Founded in 1889 with input from Old Tom Morris and later shaped by Harry Vardon and Harry Colt, the Championship Links at Royal County Down sits in a different bracket from almost any course you'll play. The Mourne Mountains rise behind you, Dundrum Bay stretches out to the front, and the course itself is relentlessly demanding: par 71, 7,186 yards, slope 142. It consistently places in the world's top five, and that ranking is not a quirk.

Green fees for visitors in 2026 are £450 for the Championship Course. That is a significant outlay, so plan around it carefully. Visitor tee times are available on Mondays, Tuesdays, Thursdays (mornings), and Fridays and Sundays (afternoons). Wednesdays and Saturdays are closed to visitors entirely. Every group must include at least one caddie, which adds to the cost but is genuinely worthwhile on a course where local knowledge saves shots. Full payment is required at booking and is non-refundable, so sort travel insurance before you commit.

The club also has the Annesley Links, a par 66 layout at 4,617 yards with a 2026 green fee of £120. It is a gentler introduction to the grounds and a useful option if you want a second day at RCD without the full outlay again.

## Ardglass Golf Club

About thirty minutes south of Newcastle along the coast, Ardglass sits on the clifftops above the Irish Sea and is one of those courses that feels entirely unlike anywhere else. The sea comes into play on eight holes and is visible from all eighteen. The clubhouse, for what it's worth, occupies a fortified building with origins dating to 1405, reputedly among the oldest structures in use as a golf clubhouse anywhere in the world.

Ardglass is significantly cheaper than Royal County Down (exact current green fees were not confirmed at the time of writing, so check directly with the club before travelling), and the quality of the experience is well above what the price suggests. For a county Down golf circuit, it sits naturally as the second day's play: accessible from Newcastle, different in character to the Championship Links, and rewarding in its own right.

## Kirkistown Castle Golf Club, Cloughey

Out on the Ards Peninsula, Kirkistown Castle is the most easterly eighteen-hole course on the island of Ireland and one of the most undervisited. The layout dates from 1934 and was designed by James Braid. At par 69 and 6,167 yards it is not long by modern standards, but the course plays on a free-draining sandy base that keeps it in good condition year-round, helped by the lowest rainfall in Northern Ireland in this part of the peninsula.

The signature hole is the par-4 10th, a 435-yard test that has a reputation as one of the toughest par fours in Irish golf. Green fees are in the region of £35 on weekdays and £40 at weekends, though these figures come from third-party aggregator data and should be verified directly before you book. The course has been recognised as the Best Value Golf Experience in Ulster, and that is not a difficult title to justify at those prices.

Belfast City Airport is roughly 45 minutes away by car, which makes Kirkistown a realistic option even on the day you fly in or out.

## Warrenpoint Golf Club

Warrenpoint is the odd one out in this circuit: a parkland course rather than links, founded in 1893, set above Carlingford Lough with the Mournes as a backdrop. Par 71, 6,173 yards, and requiring a handicap certificate for visitors. Best days for visiting are Mondays, Thursdays, Fridays, and Sundays. Green fees are approximately £38 on weekdays and £45 at weekends, again from third-party data, so worth confirming directly.

The course has hosted the Irish Professional Championship and a Ladies European Tour event, and it produced Ronan Rafferty, one of the better Irish professionals of the 1980s and 90s. It rounds out a County Down circuit nicely as the fourth course, offering a different test after three days of links golf.

## Getting There and Getting Around

Belfast City Airport (BHD) is the more convenient arrival point, sitting roughly 30 to 40 minutes from Newcastle by car. Belfast International (BFS) is about an hour. If you're travelling from mainland Britain, the ferry routes via Stena Line and P&O from Cairnryan to Belfast or Larne are worth considering, particularly if you want to bring your own clubs without airline hassle.

A car is not optional for this circuit. The courses are spread across the county and public transport links between them are not practical for a golfer carrying a bag. Newcastle makes the obvious base, with the Slieve Donard Resort directly adjacent to the Royal County Down first tee, or the Burrendale Hotel as a more mid-range alternative nearby.

## The Takeaway

The Championship Links at Royal County Down justifies the journey and the green fee on its own terms. But the county around it has four decades of golf history, clifftop links, an underrated peninsula course, and a parkland with proper pedigree, all at a fraction of the cost. Three or four days in County Down, planned properly, is one of the better golf trips available to anyone travelling from mainland Britain.
