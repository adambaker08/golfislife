There is a particular kind of stubbornness that runs through British golf. We play in conditions that would clear the course in most other countries, pull on whatever glove we happen to have in the bag, and accept that our grip will feel like wet soap by the fourth hole. It doesn't have to be that way. Understanding what to actually look for in a golf rain glove makes a more meaningful practical difference than almost any other bit of wet-weather kit, and most club golfers have never thought about it for more than thirty seconds.

## Your Standard Glove Is Doing the Wrong Thing

The reason your regular leather glove lets you down in rain is not a flaw in the design. Cabretta leather is excellent at what it does: it feels soft, conforms to the hand, and gives you a natural, responsive connection to the grip in dry or mildly humid conditions. The problem is physics. When leather absorbs water, it loses friction. The wetter it gets, the more it slides.

Synthetic all-weather gloves, like the FootJoy WeatherSof and similar styles, occupy a useful middle ground. They cope well with light drizzle and are worth having in the bag. In sustained heavy rain of the sort that arrives sideways off the Firth of Forth in October, they start to struggle too.

Dedicated rain gloves work on the opposite principle. The synthetic materials used, including fabrics like FiberSof and various forms of synthetic suede, are specifically engineered to increase grip as moisture rises. Wet hands and a soaked grip actually improve the connection rather than undermining it. Once you understand that, you start to understand why serious UK golfers who play through winter keep a separate pair specifically for the wet stuff.

## What to Actually Look for in a Golf Rain Glove

### The wet-grip material

This is the only thing that truly matters. The glove needs to be made from a material with genuine wet-grip properties, not just marketed as "water-resistant" or "all-weather." Those are not the same claim. Look for gloves positioned explicitly as rain gloves rather than all-season ones.

FootJoy's RainGrip has been the reliable choice for UK golfers for years and remains a sensible starting point. MacWet Micromesh is well-regarded, partly through association with European Tour players who wear them in proper conditions. During the 2020 Scottish Open, which took place during Storm Alex, Aaron Rai wore MacWet gloves throughout the week. Today's Golfer have cited them as a year-round winter choice. TaylorMade's Rain Control and Zero Restriction sit at different price points and are worth considering. Under Armour's Drive Storm gloves, part of their 2024 range, add another option for those who prefer that brand.

None of these figures come with prices confirmed here, because prices shift and vary by retailer. They run from budget-accessible to premium. Do not let a price point alone drive the decision without handling the glove or reading current reviews.

### They come in pairs

This catches a surprising number of golfers out. Rain gloves are sold as matched pairs, one for each hand. A standard glove is sold singly because most golfers only wear one. If you are used to buying gloves one at a time, note that the per-glove price comparison is not straightforward when you are looking at a box of two.

Wearing both gloves also matters for the technique. In wet conditions, a bare trailing hand on the grip is a weak point regardless of how good the lead glove is.

### Fit and thickness

Rain gloves tend to run slightly thicker than standard leather. Some golfers find this affects feel on the putter, which is worth knowing if you are someone who removes your glove to putt. In prolonged cold rain they can also feel chilly; this is where crossover products like Stuburt's thermal gloves become relevant, adding some warmth without sacrificing the wet-grip properties.

A snug fit matters more than it does with leather. A loose rain glove in wet conditions moves around rather than gripping the club.

## How to Use Them So They Actually Work

Rain gloves perform best when they are damp, not soaking. If you are starting a round in drizzle, wetting the gloves slightly before you tee off activates the grip faster than waiting for the rain to do it naturally.

After the round, remove the gloves and reshape them by hand. Air-dry them away from radiators or direct heat, which degrades the synthetic fibres over time. Many golfers who play regularly through winter rotate between two pairs, letting one dry fully while using the other. This extends the life of both considerably.

## The Practical Setup for UK Conditions

A sensible bag configuration for a UK golfer who plays year-round is not complicated. A good pair of leather gloves for dry days, a couple of all-weather synthetic gloves for drizzle and mild mornings, and a dedicated rain pair for when the weather turns proper. The rain gloves live in the bag and come out when the forecast gets serious or the sky makes the decision for you.

The glove is a small thing. On a soaked grip in a crosswind, it stops being a small thing very quickly.
