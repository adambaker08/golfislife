If most of your rounds are played with an electric or push trolley, buying a stand bag is a compromise from the start. Stand bags are built around the assumption that you will carry them. Cart bags are not, and that difference shapes everything from the base width to the pocket layout to how the zips are sealed.

This post is the companion piece to our guide for walking golfers. If you are a trolley-first player, this is the one for you.

## Why Cart Bags Are a Different Product Entirely

Cart bags are more structured, heavier, and offer significantly more storage than stand bags. The trade-off is straightforward: you are not carrying the weight, so the bag can afford to be roomier and more organised. Typical cart bag weights run from around 3 kg at the lighter end to 4.5 kg or more for fully featured models. Anything heavier than that should be offering features that genuinely justify it.

Because you are loading and unloading from a trolley rather than swinging the bag onto your back, the priorities shift. Pocket accessibility, base compatibility, and weatherproofing matter more here than carry comfort.

## The UK Waterproofing Question

Britain averages around 133 rain days a year. That figure alone makes the distinction between "waterproof" and "water resistant" more than a minor marketing detail.

A water-resistant bag will handle a light shower. A genuinely waterproof bag uses welded or taped seams rather than stitched ones. Stitched seams leave needle holes, and needle holes let water in. Sealed zips make the same difference on pockets. If a bag is described as waterproof but uses conventional stitching and standard zip teeth, treat the claim with scepticism.

### Rain Hoods: Still Necessary

Even a properly waterproof bag needs a rain hood. The bag body might stay dry, but exposed grips and club heads will not. Check that a hood is included (some bags sell it separately), and look for one that stows in its own dedicated pocket rather than being stuffed loosely into the main compartment.

## Trolley Compatibility Is the Structural Priority

This is the feature most buyers underestimate until they are standing in a car park trying to attach a bag that does not fit.

Most UK electric trolleys accept bags with a base width between 9 and 10 inches. Check the base measurement before you buy. Beyond width, PowaKaddy and Motocaddy both use proprietary locking systems. PowaKaddy's Mag Lock and Motocaddy's EASILOCK both prevent the bag twisting on uneven ground, which matters more than you might expect over a hilly course. If you own one of those trolleys, look for a bag with a compatible base rather than relying on the standard strap system alone.

Every cart bag should have a cart strap channel or pass-through, which allows the trolley's retaining strap to thread through the base without the bag shifting. If there is no channel, the bag will slide and rotate during transit.

## Pocket Layout: Orientation Over Quantity

Marketing materials love to lead with pocket count. Eleven pockets sounds better than seven, but if eight of those eleven require you to rotate the bag to access them, they are largely useless mid-round.

Forward-facing pockets, accessible from the playing side without moving the bag on the trolley, are what to look for. Specifically:

- **A magnetic or quick-access rangefinder pocket** at the top of the bag, reachable without bending down.
- **A valuables pocket** with a waterproof or at least water-resistant zip, ideally fleece-lined.
- **A large apparel pocket** for waterproofs, spare layers, and a towel.
- **An insulated or cooler pocket** for drinks and snacks, particularly useful on longer summer rounds.

Full-length dividers matter too. A 14-way divider with full-length separators keeps clubs from tangling during trolley travel, which is a genuine irritation over 18 holes on bumpy ground.

## Weight and the Carry Handle

The bag sits on the trolley for most of the round, so overall weight is less critical here than it would be for a carry bag. That said, you will still lift it in and out of the car, on and off the trolley, and occasionally across a car park. Something in the 3 to 3.5 kg range is noticeably easier to handle than a 4.5 kg model, even if you never carry it for a full hole.

Check that there is a proper carry handle, not just a narrow strap. A reinforced or rubberised base also helps: it prevents wear on wet grass and hard cart paths, and it keeps the bag stable when you set it down.

## The Practical Shortlist Before You Buy

Before committing to a cart bag, run through these quickly:

1. Does the base width match your trolley?
2. Is it compatible with your trolley's locking system if you use PowaKaddy or Motocaddy?
3. Does it have welded or taped seams if you want genuine waterproofing?
4. Are the key pockets forward-facing and accessible from the playing side?
5. Is the rain hood included?
6. Does the weight feel manageable for loading and unloading, not just for rolling?

Get those six right and the rest, colour, style, divider count, becomes a matter of personal preference rather than function.

The cart bag market is well served in the UK at the moment. The main mistake most buyers make is treating it like a stand bag purchase and prioritising how it looks over how it works when mounted on a trolley. On a wet Tuesday in October, you will be glad you did not.
