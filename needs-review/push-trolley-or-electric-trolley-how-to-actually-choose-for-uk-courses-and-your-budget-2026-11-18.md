Most UK golfers who've decided to stop carrying face the same question almost immediately: do you spend somewhere around £150–£200 on a decent push trolley, or do you stretch to three, four, even five times that for an electric? Both camps have vocal advocates, and the marketing from trolley brands doesn't exactly help you cut through it.

This guide doesn't declare a winner. The honest answer is that the right choice depends on a handful of factors specific to you, and once you map those honestly, the decision usually makes itself.

## The Core Difference (Beyond the Obvious)

Yes, one has a motor and one doesn't. But the practical gap goes further than that.

Push trolleys are lighter, fold more compactly, and typically come with more storage built in as standard: umbrella holder, drinks holder, scorecard slot. Electric models often treat those as paid extras. Push trolleys also ask very little of you logistically. You unfold them, clip your bag on, and walk.

Electric trolleys are heavier, because the motor and battery hardware adds real bulk. That matters not just on the course but when you're heaving it in and out of your car boot round after round. If you're evaluating an electric model, how smoothly and compactly it folds is worth paying more attention to than the spec sheet.

## The Four Questions That Actually Decide It

### 1. What are your courses like?

This is the biggest one. If you regularly play hilly, undulating courses, an electric trolley pays its way very quickly in terms of effort saved and pace of play. You're not fighting against the gradient on every other hole.

On flatter, parkland-style courses, a push trolley is genuinely comfortable for most golfers over 18 holes. The effort required is modest, especially on well-maintained fairways.

If you play a mix, think about where you play most. That course should drive the decision.

### 2. How often do you actually play?

An electric trolley is a bigger investment to justify if you're getting out eight or ten times a year. If you're playing twice a week through the season, the cost-per-round calculation looks very different, and the convenience compounds quickly.

Push trolleys have almost no running costs and minimal maintenance. Electric trolleys require battery care, occasional servicing, and eventual battery replacement, which is worth factoring into the longer-term picture.

### 3. What's your storage and transport situation?

A push trolley will fit in most boots without drama. Electric trolleys, even with compact fold mechanisms, take up considerably more space and weigh more to lift. If you're sharing a car, have a small boot, or store your gear in a flat or tight garage, this is a more important constraint than it might seem when you're standing in a shop.

### 4. What's your honest budget?

Push trolley quality starts to get genuinely good from around £150. Models like the PowaKaddy Micra (around £189) and the Clicgear 4.0 (around £219) are cited across recent buyer guides as reliable, well-built options that golfers are still using after multiple seasons.

On the electric side, entry-level models start around £499, mid-range options sit in the region of £749, and flagship models with GPS touchscreens can reach £1,249 or more. Remote and follow-style models from brands like Stewart Golf push further still.

That's not to say electric trolleys aren't worth the money if the factors above point that way. But the jump is real, and it's worth being clear-eyed about it rather than stretching because you feel you should.

## A Quick Word on Batteries

If you're going electric, lithium battery is the standard to look for. It's lighter and longer-lasting than older lead-acid technology. Standard lithium capacity will cover a typical 18-hole round; if you play longer rounds, hilly courses, or back-to-back days, it's worth looking at extended-capacity options.

## Brands Worth Looking At

For push trolleys, PowaKaddy and Motocaddy both have solid UK market presence, and Clicgear is well regarded for build quality. At the more compact end, the Motocaddy Z1 is noted for its fold.

For electric trolleys, Motocaddy and PowaKaddy dominate the mainstream UK market, with Hill Billy worth a mention as a UK-built alternative. At the premium end, Stewart Golf occupies a different bracket. All of these are informational mentions; there are no affiliate links in this post.

## The Takeaway

If you play frequently, tackle hilly courses regularly, and have the boot space and budget to match, an electric trolley is a genuinely comfortable upgrade that you'll appreciate every round. If you play less often, mostly walk flat-to-moderate courses, or want something simple and cost-effective, a well-made push trolley is not a compromise. For a lot of UK golfers on a lot of UK courses, it's simply the right tool.

The mistake is letting either camp talk you into their answer before you've thought honestly about yours.
