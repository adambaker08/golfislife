If you've spent any time browsing golf forums lately, you'd be forgiven for thinking the electric trolley versus push trolley debate has already been settled in favour of electric. It hasn't. For a lot of UK golfers playing specific types of courses, a well-made push trolley remains the smarter choice. This guide is about helping you figure out which side of the line you actually fall on, rather than defaulting to the assumption that spending more means choosing better.

## The basics worth getting straight first

Pull trolleys, the old two-wheeled kind you drag behind you, are largely worth ignoring. The arm position and twisting motion they require puts unnecessary strain on your body over 18 holes. Both push and electric trolleys use a three-wheel design you walk behind and push, and that's a better starting point regardless of which type you end up choosing.

Within electric trolleys there are a few sub-types: manual-steer (you walk behind and steer it yourself), remote-controlled (you direct it from a distance as you walk), and follow systems (the trolley tracks you autonomously). The price rises significantly as you move through those categories. The most common choice for UK golfers is still manual-steer electric, and that's what most of the decision below is based on.

## Where you play matters more than you might think

This is probably the single biggest factor in the electric or push trolley question for UK golfers, and it's specific enough that generic guides often miss it.

**Hilly inland parkland and heathland courses** are where electric trolleys earn their keep. Pushing a trolley loaded with clubs up a long slope, repeatedly, across 18 holes does take it out of you. An electric trolley removes that effort entirely. Some models also include electronic braking systems (often labelled EBS or DHC depending on the brand) that automatically slow the trolley on steep downhill sections, which matters on courses with sharp drops.

**Flat coastal links courses** are a different matter. Walking a relatively level seaside layout, even in a stiff breeze, doesn't place the same physical demands on your legs. A decent push trolley handles that kind of terrain with very little effort from you. The extra cost and weight of an electric model don't earn their place in quite the same way.

If you regularly play both types, that's when the case for electric strengthens, since it covers both situations without compromise.

## The cost gap is real and worth being honest about

Entry-level electric trolleys from the two dominant UK brands, PowaKaddy and Motocaddy, sit at roughly £500 for a basic manual-steer model. Mid-range compact electric options are around £550. Models with built-in GPS and automatic downhill braking push toward £1,000 and beyond. These figures are approximate and based on published guides at the time of writing; prices do change, so treat them as a guide to the tier relationships rather than guaranteed current prices.

By comparison, a quality three-wheel push trolley from PowaKaddy or Clicgear costs somewhere in the £190 to £220 range. Premium push options like the Stewart R1-S sit higher than that, but still well below the electric tier.

For a golfer playing twice a month on a fairly flat course, that price gap is hard to justify on performance grounds alone. For someone playing hilly courses three or four times a week, the electric model's cost can feel reasonable across a few seasons of use.

## Boot space and weight: underrated practical concerns

Electric trolleys are heavier. The motor and battery add meaningful weight, and even a well-folded electric trolley is more of a lift in and out of the boot than a push model. If you drive a smaller car, this is worth thinking about before you buy. Push trolleys generally fold more compactly and weigh considerably less, which also makes them easier to manage if you're walking to the course or using a club storage facility.

On battery: if you do go electric, pay attention to the battery capacity. A standard 18-hole lithium battery covers a normal round, but hillier courses or back-to-back days of golf benefit from a 27- or 36-hole rating. Lithium batteries are preferable to older lead-acid types on weight and longevity grounds.

## A rough decision framework

**Lean towards electric if:**
- You regularly play hilly, demanding courses
- You're walking 36 holes in a day or playing several days in a row
- Fatigue across a round is genuinely affecting your back nine
- Your car boot and budget can handle it comfortably

**Lean towards push if:**
- Your regular courses are relatively flat
- You play once a week or less
- Boot space or the weight of lifting the trolley in and out is a real concern
- You'd rather put the cost difference toward something else

## The actual takeaway

Electric is not automatically the better trolley. It's the better trolley for specific situations. If you play undulating parkland or heathland courses regularly, and you've got the budget and boot space, it's a straightforward upgrade. If you play a flat links twice a month, a solid push trolley does the job well and leaves several hundred pounds in your pocket. Work out your course first, then choose accordingly.
