## The Bit Most Golfers Miss

There's a stretch of Scotland's north-east coast that sits in a curious blind spot. Golfers driving up through the Highlands tend to fix their eyes on Royal Dornoch to the north or St Andrews to the south, and the Moray Firth golf coast gets overlooked entirely. That's a genuine mistake. The region packs in a championship links with a serious history, an Old Tom Morris original that's also one of the quirkiest courses in Britain, and several character courses that are simply excellent fun to play. Spread over three or four days, it makes for a self-contained trip that costs considerably less than the usual Scottish pilgrimage circuits.

The geography helps. The Firth sits roughly midway between Inverness and Aberdeen, which makes Inverness the natural arrival airport and the Lossiemouth and Elgin area a sensible base. Courses run in a logical coastal sequence, so you're driving forward rather than doubling back. The region also has a reputation for a favourable microclimate. October, in particular, is worth considering: the courses are quieter, green fees at some clubs come down, and the light along the Firth on a clear autumn morning is difficult to argue with.

## The Anchor Courses

### Nairn Golf Club

Nairn is the most prominent name on the Moray Firth golf coast and it earns the status. Founded in 1887 and shaped over the years by Archie Simpson, Old Tom Morris and James Braid, it's a championship links that has hosted the Walker Cup, the Curtis Cup, the Amateur Championship and the Women's Amateur. That's not a thin CV.

Golfshake lists a guide green fee of around £230 for the Championship Course in high season, though that figure should be confirmed directly with the club before you build a budget around it. It's not cheap, but the condition and the pedigree justify the spend if a marquee links round is what you're after. The neighbouring Nairn Dunbar Golf Club is a more affordable alternative and is worth including in an itinerary if you want to play the town twice without repeating the same experience.

### Moray Golf Club, Lossiemouth

Founded in 1889, Moray at Lossiemouth has two full 18-hole links courses. The Old Course was laid out by Old Tom Morris; the New Course was designed by Sir Henry Cotton. Both are ranked among Scotland's best, and the setup has a pleasing resemblance to St Andrews in the way the course weaves in and out of the town itself. The closing hole on the Old Course is widely regarded as one of the finest finishing holes in British links golf. Green fees are competitive with other serious Scottish links, and you can reasonably play both courses across two days without it feeling like a repeat exercise.

## The Courses That Make the Trip

### Hopeman Golf Club

Hopeman is the sort of place that rewards golfers who do a bit of digging before they go. Extended to 18 holes in 1985, it's a proper, demanding links with gorse and broom guarding the fairways throughout. The signature hole is the 12th, a par 3 called The Prieshach, which plays down into Clashach Cove roughly 150 feet below the tee. Paul Lawrie, the club's Honorary President, has described it as probably the best par 3 he has ever played; Sky Golf has listed it among the best par 3s in Scotland. On a clear day, you can spot dolphins in the Firth from the tee, which is not something most par 3s offer.

Green fees are affordable, the clubhouse is known as The Mashie Niblick, and it serves Cullen Skink alongside home-cooked food. It's a comfortable place to spend time after a round.

### Cullen Links

Cullen might be the most idiosyncratic course on this stretch. Originally laid out by Old Tom Morris in 1870 as nine holes and later extended to 18 by local architect Charlie Neaves, it sits beneath red sandstone cliffs in the harbour town of Cullen. At just over 4,600 yards, it's recognised as one of the shortest true links courses in the world, and the layout contains ten par 3s. Holes 12 and 13 play over an 80-foot rock formation called Boar Crag. It's now part of the Old Tom Morris Trail. The whole thing is quirky, scenic, and great value, and it offers something genuinely different from anywhere else on the route.

## Rounding Out the Itinerary

If you're spending several days in the area, Elgin Golf Club is a useful addition as an inland contrast. Founded in 1906 and measuring over 6,400 yards, it's regarded as one of the finer inland courses in northern Scotland, and it gives the legs and the concentration a slightly different kind of test.

For those arriving via Inverness, Fortrose and Rosemarkie is only about 20 minutes from the airport and makes an excellent first-day option. The course sits on a narrow peninsula into the Firth, and dolphin sightings are a genuine possibility.

For joining up green fees across the region, it's worth checking the Moray Firth Golf Pass (morayfirthgolf.co.uk), which offers discounts across local courses. The specifics of what's included change, so review it against your planned itinerary rather than assuming full coverage.

## The Honest Case for Going

The Moray Firth golf coast won't replace Royal Dornoch for the golfer who has Dornoch on their list. It doesn't need to. What it offers is a coherent, varied golf trip through a region that most visiting golfers skip, at a price point (across most of the courses) that leaves some room in the budget. Nairn gives you the prestige round. Moray at Lossiemouth gives you history and proper links golf over two days. Hopeman and Cullen give you the kind of experience that you'll still be talking about on the drive south. That's a decent return from one stretch of coastline.
