There is no shortage of opinions on this one. Ask in the clubhouse and you will get a different answer from every person at the table. The electric vs push golf trolley decision is one of those purchases that feels simple until you start pulling at the threads, and then suddenly you are knee-deep in battery ratings, fold dimensions, and whether your boot can actually fit the thing.

This guide cuts through the noise. It is aimed at the regular UK walker: someone who gets out most weeks, plays a mix of course types, and wants to make a sensible decision rather than just buying whatever is on the pro shop shelf.

## Why This Decision Is Different in the UK

Terrain matters enormously here, and it is the factor most buying guides ignore because they are written for a generic global audience.

A flat parkland course in the Midlands is a very different proposition from a links on the Fife coast or a heathland track that climbs and drops every other hole. If your home course has real gradient to it, the balance of this decision tilts noticeably. Pushing a loaded trolley up a long slope, round after round, adds up. That physical cost is real, and it is worth being honest about it before defaulting to the cheaper option.

Damp conditions are the other UK-specific factor. Wet grass, muddy paths, soft fairways in winter: a push trolley requires you to put in the effort regardless of conditions underfoot, while an electric motor handles the gradient for you. Neither type is waterproof as such, but an electric trolley at least means your arms and back are not absorbing the extra resistance a wet course creates.

## The Case for a Three-Wheel Push Trolley

If you are playing less frequently, or your course is on the flatter side, a good push trolley is genuinely hard to argue against.

The practical advantages are real. Push trolleys fold more compactly than electric models because there is no motor or battery pack adding bulk. If your car boot is tight, or storage at home is limited, this matters. They are also lighter to lift in and out of the car, which is a small but cumulative benefit over a season.

Three-wheel models are the right choice here. The old-fashioned two-wheel pull trolley, where you drag the bag behind you on one side, puts uneven strain on the body over eighteen holes. Pushing a three-wheel trolley in front of you is a more natural movement and distributes the load far better. It is worth avoiding two-wheelers entirely if you are walking regularly.

Storage is another genuine push trolley advantage. Many electric trolley designs sacrifice storage space to accommodate the motor and battery, meaning accessories like umbrella holders and drink holders often have to be bought separately. Push trolleys tend to offer more out of the box.

## The Case for Going Electric

If you walk at least once a week and your course has any meaningful gradient, the electric trolley argument gets strong quickly.

Motocaddy and PowaKaddy dominate the UK electric market. The Motocaddy S1 and PowaKaddy CT6 are among the most cited models at the entry-to-mid level. At the top end, GPS-integrated models such as the PowaKaddy CT12 add distance information and course mapping. Stewart Golf's Q Follow uses Bluetooth to track and follow you hands-free, which is a different category of experience altogether.

Features worth looking for on any electric trolley:

- **Downhill control.** Motocaddy call theirs DHC. It prevents the trolley rolling away from you on descents. On a proper hilly course, this goes from nice-to-have to essential.
- **Battery range.** Entry-level models are typically rated to 18 holes. Mid and upper models push to 36. If you are playing back-to-back days or do not want to charge after every round, this is worth checking.
- **Adjustable handle height.** Non-negotiable for comfort across a full round.
- **A foot brake.** Useful any time you park the trolley on a slope or near a raised tee.

The practical downsides: electric trolleys need charging between rounds, and the battery should be stored dry. For many UK golfers, that means a garage or utility room, which is worth thinking through before buying. They also fold less compactly, so measure your boot before committing.

Prices across the category run from roughly £500 at the entry-level end of the established brands up to over £1,200 for GPS flagship models. These figures shift with retailers and seasons, so treat them as a rough orientation rather than a shopping list.

## How to Make the Call

Run through these honestly:

**Choose electric if:** you walk at least weekly, your home course is undulating, or you want to take the physical load off for health reasons (keeping advice general here: if you have any specific concerns, that is a conversation for your GP, not a gear guide).

**Choose push if:** you play less often, your course is relatively flat, boot space is tight, or you want to keep the outlay lower and the kit simpler.

**Either way:** buy a three-wheel model. The two-wheel pull trolley has had its day.

The right answer genuinely does depend on your specific course and how often you are out there. Neither option is objectively better for every golfer. What matters is matching the kit to your actual game, not to the most impressive spec sheet.
