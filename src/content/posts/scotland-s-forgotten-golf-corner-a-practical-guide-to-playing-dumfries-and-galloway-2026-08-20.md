Every year, thousands of golfers pile up the A77 to Ayrshire or head east to Gullane and North Berwick. A smaller number make the long haul to the Highlands. Almost none of them turn left at the M74 and head down into Dumfries and Galloway. That is their loss, and at current prices, arguably your gain.

Scotland's south-western corner, where the Solway Firth opens out towards the Cumbrian coast and the hills of Galloway roll inland, holds a collection of links and coastal courses that would generate serious waiting lists if they sat further north. They don't. Tee times are available. Green fees are reasonable. The golf is, in several cases, genuinely excellent.

This is a practical guide to planning a dedicated trip to Dumfries and Galloway, the most consistently overlooked golf region in Scotland.

## Why It Gets Bypassed

The region doesn't have a Turnberry or a St Andrews to anchor it in the public imagination. It lacks a major tour event in the calendar. Getting there from England requires bypassing, on the map at least, the more famous Ayrshire names. Golfers heading north tend to lock onto a famous name and drive past everything else.

What Dumfries and Galloway does have: the fingerprints of James Braid, Mackenzie Ross, and Willie Fernie across several of its courses, roughly 200 miles of coastline, a Gulf Stream climate that keeps the far south-west playable year-round, and greens fees that look like a misprint compared with what you'd pay on the Ayrshire coast.

## The Courses Worth Building a Trip Around

### Southerness: The One You Go For First

Southerness is the flagship, and it earns the title. Mackenzie Ross, the architect who rebuilt Turnberry after the Second World War, designed the course, which opened in 1947 on the Solway coast. It runs to 6,728 yards, plays to a par of 73, and sits flat enough that there are no real hills to negotiate between holes. The views across the Solway Firth towards the Cumbrian fells and the Galloway Hills are, on a clear day, genuinely arresting.

It is ranked 36th in Scotland nationally. The green fee in 2026 is £95. For context, a round at a comparable-quality course on the Ayrshire coast would cost you considerably more. Visitors who are members of a recognised golf club are welcome, and booking is done through the BRS system online or by phone via the club directly.

If you only have time for one course in the region, it is Southerness.

### Powfoot: The Ideal Second Round

Powfoot, founded in 1903, sits on undulating links land beside the Solway Firth. The layout has a links character for most of its length, with the final four holes shifting into more parkland territory. It is ranked 76th in Scotland. The 2026 green fee is £40.

Put Southerness and Powfoot together in a single trip and you have played two top-100 ranked Scottish golf courses for under £150 in green fees. That combination is difficult to match anywhere in Scotland at this price point.

Dumfries makes a sensible base for both courses, with Southerness around 15 miles to the south and Powfoot a similar distance to the west.

### Portpatrick (Dunskey Course): Clifftop Golf with a View to Ireland

Portpatrick sits on the far western tip of the region, about as close to Ireland as it is to Edinburgh. The Dunskey course was founded in 1903, originally laid out by Charles Hunter (professional at Prestwick), extended to 18 holes in 1913, and now plays at 5,913 yards, par 70, from around 150 feet above sea level on the clifftop above the village.

The character is rolling moorland and seaside heath rather than true links. On a clear day you can see Ireland 22 miles across the water, along with the Isle of Man, the Mourne Mountains, and the Mull of Kintyre. The 13th hole, known as Sandeel, is frequently picked out for its panoramic outlook.

Green fees at Portpatrick are visitor-friendly, though confirmed figures weren't available at time of writing, so check directly with the club before travelling. There is also a 9-hole par-3 Dinvin course on site, useful for loosening up or working on your short game.

The Gulf Stream climate here is genuinely mild by Scottish standards, and the course is playable throughout the winter months.

### Wigtownshire County: Worth the Detour

On the shores of Luce Bay near Glenluce, Wigtownshire County started as a 9-holer in 1894 and was extended to 18 holes in 1987. It carries a links character and the sea breezes off the bay make club selection interesting. It tops the regional rankings on Leading Courses with a rating of 9.0 out of 10, and green fees are in the modest, visitor-friendly bracket. Again, confirm directly with the club.

Portpatrick village itself is a small harbour town well set up for a short golf break, and using it as a base covers both Dunskey and Wigtownshire County without much driving.

## Getting There and Practical Logistics

Most UK visitors drive. There is no major airport serving the region. From Glasgow or Edinburgh, you are looking at roughly 1.5 to 2 hours by car depending on your exact destination. Dumfries is the logical hub for the eastern part of the region; Portpatrick village suits the western courses.

A logical structure for a three-night trip would be two nights based in or near Dumfries (Southerness and Powfoot), then move west and finish with a night in Portpatrick (Dunskey and Wigtownshire County).

## The Honest Case for Going

This is not a region trying to be something it isn't. The courses here are genuinely good, not good for somewhere quiet or good for the price. Southerness at £95 would be considered reasonable value if it sat beside Turnberry. Powfoot at £40 is a bargain by any Scottish standard.

If you have been putting off a Scottish links trip because everywhere seems expensive and crowded, Dumfries and Galloway is a straightforward answer to both problems.
