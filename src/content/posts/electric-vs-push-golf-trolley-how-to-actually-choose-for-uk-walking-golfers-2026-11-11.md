Trolley guides have a habit of either steering you straight to electric ("treat yourself, it's worth it") or sitting so firmly on the fence they tell you nothing useful. If you're a regular UK walking golfer trying to make a sensible decision, neither of those helps much.

So here's a more direct take. The electric vs push golf trolley question does have a real answer, it just depends on a handful of specific things about how and where you play. Work through each one honestly and the right choice usually becomes obvious.

## The Four Things That Actually Decide It

### 1. How hilly is your course?

This is the single biggest factor, and it's the one most guides gloss over.

On a genuinely hilly parkland course, pushing a loaded trolley up repeated slopes for four hours is tiring work. It adds to your fatigue in a way that affects your game. Electric trolleys remove that effort almost entirely, and the better ones include an electronic braking system (EBS) that holds the trolley steady on descents rather than letting it run away from you. If your home course has serious elevation, EBS is not a luxury feature.

On a flatter course, a push trolley handles perfectly well. Many links and heathland courses are more undulating than truly steep, and a good three-wheel push trolley rolls smoothly enough that the physical effort is modest. If you regularly walk a course with minimal gradient, the case for electric becomes much weaker.

Be honest about your course. "A few slopes" is not the same as the kind of sustained hilliness that justifies the outlay.

### 2. How often do you play?

If you're out two or three times a week throughout the season, an electric trolley starts to make a lot of sense. The convenience compounds, the battery pays for itself in effort saved, and the wear on a push trolley adds up faster than you'd expect at that frequency.

If you play once a fortnight, or mostly in the summer months, a push trolley is likely more than adequate. You're not accumulating enough rounds for the electric convenience to outweigh the cost and storage considerations.

### 3. What's your car boot situation?

Electric trolleys are heavier and bulkier than push trolleys, full stop. The motor and battery add size that doesn't disappear even when folded. If you drive a smaller car or regularly share boot space, this matters.

Push trolleys fold down considerably more compactly. The Clicgear 4.0 is a decent example of a sturdy three-wheeler that folds into a manageable package, and the Motocaddy Cube takes compact folding further still, designed specifically to take up minimal space.

Before you buy anything, measure your boot and check the folded dimensions of whichever model you're considering. It's a mundane step that saves real frustration.

### 4. What's your actual budget?

Push trolleys occupy a sensible price range. Decent options from PowaKaddy and others sit around £150 to £220. That's a straightforward outlay with no ongoing costs beyond the occasional replacement part.

Electric trolleys start at roughly £500 for entry-level models (Motocaddy's SE range is the most cited starting point in the UK market) and climb to over £1,200 for GPS-integrated top-end options from PowaKaddy and Motocaddy. Remote-control models, where the trolley follows a signal rather than being pushed or steered, sit in a mid-to-upper price bracket; the Motocaddy ME Remote is the most accessible entry point in that segment. Full auto-follow trolleys (like the Stewart Q Follow) are at a noticeably higher price point still and remain a niche choice for most club golfers.

Worth noting: electric trolleys have a split warranty structure on most models, covering the frame and battery separately. Check both terms before you buy, particularly the battery, since that's the component that will eventually need replacing.

## A Few Points Worth Knowing Before You Commit

**Battery type matters.** Lithium is now standard on modern electric trolleys and is the right choice: lighter, faster to charge, longer lifespan than older lead-acid options. If you're buying new, you'll almost certainly be getting lithium, but it's worth confirming on any discounted older stock.

**Match battery capacity to your round length.** An 18-hole battery is fine for a typical club golfer. If you regularly play 27 or 36 holes in a day, or your course is particularly long and hilly, a larger capacity battery is worth specifying. This information is in every model's listed spec.

**Push trolleys typically have more storage.** Electric trolleys sacrifice accessory storage to accommodate the battery and motor housing. If you like a trolley loaded with pockets for balls, tees, rangefinder, jacket and snacks, push options often serve that better.

**Walking is the right choice.** Both trolley types assume you're walking, which remains the norm at most UK clubs. That's a good thing, and a trolley of either kind makes it more sustainable over the course of a full round.

## The Short Version

If your course is hilly, you play regularly, and your budget stretches to £500 or more, an electric trolley will likely improve your game by the back nine. If your course is relatively flat, you play casually, storage space is tight, or you'd rather not spend that much, a quality push trolley does the job without complication.

Neither is a bad choice. The mistake is letting the showroom appeal of electric kit override a clear look at your own circumstances.
