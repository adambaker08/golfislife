There is no universally correct answer to the electric or push trolley question, but there is almost certainly a correct answer for you, based on where you play, how often you play, and what you actually want from a round of golf. This guide is about working that out, not listing spec sheets.

## The question nobody asks first: what are your courses actually like?

Terrain is the single biggest factor, and it is where most buying decisions go wrong. People read a glowing review of a premium electric trolley and buy it for a flat coastal links course where a decent push model would have served them just as well for a fraction of the price. The reverse also happens.

If your home course, or the courses you play most, involve sustained climbs, awkward cambers, or soft fairways that become heavy going in autumn and winter, an electric trolley earns its keep quickly. The motor handles the gradient, your arms stay fresher, and you are more likely to actually enjoy the back nine. Downhill control, a feature found on models like the Motocaddy M5 GPS DHC and comparable PowaKaddy options, is not just marketing language on UK courses. Soft ground and steep descents are a genuine combination, and a trolley that holds itself back rather than running away from you makes a real difference.

If your regular course is relatively flat, whether that is a municipal parkland or a links-style track without serious elevation, a three-wheel push trolley from a reputable brand will do the job. Models from Clicgear, Big Max, and Motocaddy's QB2 range are built properly, roll cleanly, and ask nothing of you between rounds except occasionally wiping them down. No charging, no battery to manage, no gearbox to wear out.

## The honest cost picture

Electric trolleys start at somewhere above £500 for a basic entry-level model and climb past £1,000 for premium GPS-integrated options. Push trolleys from quality brands typically sit below £200. That gap is significant, and the question is whether the comfort and effort saving justifies it over the number of rounds you are actually going to play.

If you are out twice a month on a moderate course, the honest answer is probably no. If you play weekly or more through winter on demanding ground, the calculation changes. A season of easier rounds adds up.

One thing worth factoring in that rarely appears in buying guides: the weight of lifting the trolley itself. Electric models carry a motor, battery, and gearbox, and that total weight is real when you are loading and unloading a car boot every week. If you drive something with a high tailgate and limited space, the difference between a folded push trolley and a folded electric model is noticeable. Both categories now produce compact-fold designs, but push trolleys generally win on boot-friendliness.

## A few practical details that actually matter

**Battery.** Lithium is now standard on reputable electric models. A standard 18-hole battery covers a normal round comfortably. If you regularly play hillier ground or occasionally do back-to-back rounds, it is worth checking whether a 27- or 36-hole battery option exists for the model you are considering.

**Wheel size on push trolleys.** This matters more than most buyers realise. Larger rear wheels, in the 10 to 12 inch range, handle soft and muddy ground considerably better than smaller 7 to 8 inch wheels. If you are playing through a wet UK winter, wheel diameter on a push trolley is worth checking before you buy.

**Features to skip unless you will genuinely use them.** GPS screens built into the trolley handle and remote or follow modes are available at the premium end of electric models, but they come with a meaningful price jump. If you already use a GPS watch or phone app, a handle-mounted screen duplicates something you have. Remote and follow modes are clever, but unless your course specifically suits them, they add cost and complexity without much practical return for most club golfers.

**Storage between rounds.** An electric trolley should be stored dry and kept charged according to the battery manufacturer's guidance. This is not complicated, but it is an ongoing consideration that a push trolley simply does not require.

## Which way does the decision actually point?

If you play at least weekly on undulating or hilly UK parkland, an electric trolley with downhill control and an all-terrain tyre setup is a genuinely sensible investment. You will feel the difference by mid-season.

If you play less frequently, play relatively flat courses, or have genuine boot-space constraints, a well-built three-wheel push trolley is not a compromise. It is the right tool for the job. The old two-wheel pull carts that people associate with the word "trolley" are largely irrelevant now. Modern three-wheel push designs put the weight ahead of you, which is a much more natural movement and significantly easier on the body over 18 holes.

The decision comes down to this: be honest about your course, your playing frequency, and your car. Answer those three things clearly and the choice tends to make itself.
