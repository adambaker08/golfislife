There are few decisions in golf that feel more mundane and turn out to be more personal than how you move your bag around the course. Electric or push trolley: it sounds simple, but get it wrong and you will either be lugging a battery-powered behemoth up a slope you could have handled yourself, or grinding through a hilly heathland round wishing something else was doing the work. This guide is about helping you land on the right category first, before you start comparing individual models.

## Why the Old Pull Trolley Deserves a Quiet Retirement

Before anything else: if you are still using a single-handle pull trolley dragged behind you, it is worth reconsidering. That motion puts uneven strain on one side of the body across eighteen holes, and the two-handle, three-wheel push trolley has largely replaced it as the modern manual standard. This post is about push versus electric, not pull versus everything else, but the distinction is worth making upfront.

## The Core Question: What Are Your Courses Actually Like?

This is where most trolley comparisons go wrong by being too general. The honest answer to "electric or push?" depends heavily on the terrain you are walking.

On flatter parkland or links courses, a good push trolley does the job without fuss. You are applying effort on a relatively even surface, and the gap in exertion between pushing and not pushing is manageable for most reasonably fit club golfers.

On hilly heathland, undulating parkland, or any course where you are climbing and descending repeatedly, an electric motor starts earning its keep properly. The energy you save across a full round is real and cumulative. Some Motocaddy models include a feature called Downhill Control, which automatically regulates the trolley's speed on descents rather than leaving you to hold it back manually. On a genuinely steep course, that is a practical benefit rather than a marketing add-on.

If your home course is fairly flat but you occasionally play hillier tracks, that changes the calculation again. Be honest about your typical round, not your best-case scenario.

## How Often Do You Actually Play?

An electric trolley carries a noticeably higher upfront cost than a push equivalent. Entry-level electrics exist, but even those represent a meaningful step up from a decent push trolley. The more frequently you play, the more that cost per round comes down over time.

For golfers playing once or twice a week through a full season, the outlay for electric is easier to justify. For someone playing twenty rounds a year, a well-made push trolley is likely the more sensible choice, unless the terrain question above tips the balance.

## Boot Space, Storage, and the Weight Problem

Electric trolleys are heavier. The motor and battery hardware add real weight, which matters most when you are lifting a folded trolley in and out of a car boot. Modern designs fold down considerably smaller than older generations, but the weight is still there.

If you drive something with a small boot or regularly squeeze your kit into a shared car for a society day, a push trolley is simply easier to live with. Lightweight push options like the Big Max Blade IP 2, which uses a flat-fold mechanism to reduce its packed footprint, or the PowaKaddy DLX-Lite FF are genuinely compact once folded.

For electric, the fold mechanism matters more than it does on a push trolley precisely because you are lifting more weight each time. It is worth checking how a model folds and how it sits in the boot before buying, not just how it looks on a course.

## A Note on Batteries for UK Conditions

If you do go electric, battery capacity is worth thinking about in specifically UK terms. An 18-hole lithium battery covers a standard round fine in reasonable conditions. On hillier courses, or if you play back-to-back rounds at a golf break, a larger battery designed for 27 or 36 holes provides useful headroom. Cold weather also affects battery performance, which is a practical consideration for anyone playing through a British winter.

## The Premium End: Follow Trolleys

At the top of the electric category sit follow or remote-control trolleys. Models like the Stewart Golf Q Follow use Bluetooth to trail the golfer hands-free, which is as close to a caddy experience as a trolley gets. The Motocaddy M7 GPS Remote adds a touchscreen GPS unit and remote control to the package. These are worth knowing about, but they carry a premium price to match the features, and for most club golfers they represent more than is needed.

## A Middle Ground Worth Knowing About

If neither category feels quite right, there is a small but growing hybrid segment. The MGI E-Boost Push Cart, for example, sits between a traditional push trolley and a fully electric model by adding a degree of motor assistance without taking over entirely. It is not for everyone, but it is worth being aware of if you like the idea of occasional help on climbs without committing to a full electric setup.

## Which One Is Actually Right for You

Run through these honestly:

- **Hilly home course?** Lean towards electric.
- **Flat course, occasional play?** A good push trolley is probably enough.
- **Small car, shared travel to away days?** Push is easier.
- **Playing twice a week or more?** Electric starts to justify itself.
- **Budget is a real constraint?** Push trolleys offer better value at the same quality tier.

The best trolley is the one that suits where you actually play, how often you play, and how it fits into your life off the course. Neither category is universally better. Getting that choice right matters more than which specific model you pick within it.
