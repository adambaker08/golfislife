County Down is one of the most links-rich corners of the British Isles, yet it gets treated by most visiting golfers as a one-course destination. Fly in, play Royal County Down, fly home. That's understandable — RCD is as good as golf gets — but it means most people leave two genuinely distinctive links courses completely unplayed. Ardglass and Kirkistown Castle are not consolation prizes. They are courses worth crossing the Irish Sea for in their own right.

This guide lays out a practical three-day circuit of County Down, treating RCD as the headline act it deserves to be, while making sure the supporting cast gets a proper introduction.

## Why County Down Works as a Golf Circuit

The geography helps enormously. Belfast sits at the top of the county with two airports. Royal County Down is roughly 45 minutes south on the A24, with Newcastle as its base. Ardglass is about 30 minutes further round the coast. Kirkistown Castle sits on the Ards Peninsula, around 45 minutes from Belfast on the A20. None of these are long drives, which means you can base yourself in one place and reach all three courses without losing half the day to travel.

The sensible order is also, roughly, ascending order of green fee — which is a convenient way to build up to the main event.

## Day One: Kirkistown Castle

Start at Kirkistown Castle Golf Club in Cloughey on the Ards Peninsula. It holds the distinction of being the most easterly 18-hole course on the island of Ireland, and it's the nearest true links course to Belfast, which makes it the logical warm-up for the days ahead.

The course was laid out by James Braid in 1934 and has changed little since. At around 6,167 yards off the medal tees, it's not long by modern standards, but links golf here is about the terrain, the wind off the Irish Sea, and what you do with both. The ground drains quickly enough that the course is rarely closed after rain, which matters in October and shoulder-season windows when conditions elsewhere can be more uncertain.

Green fees are modest by the standards of the circuit (approximate figures available via Golfshake or the club directly — check current rates before booking as prices change seasonally). Visitors are welcome seven days a week, which gives you real flexibility.

The signature hole is the par-4 10th, a 435-yard test that has a reputation as one of the tougher par 4s in Irish golf.

## Day Two: Ardglass

Ardglass Golf Club sits on cliffs above the Irish Sea, roughly halfway along the County Down coast. Founded in 1896, it plays to par 70 over around 6,216 yards, and the Irish Sea comes into play — properly into play, not as a distant backdrop — on eight of those holes.

The opening stretch is the most immediately dramatic. The par-3 2nd crosses a ravine, and several early holes are separated from jagged rocks and sea by what feels like very little margin. Views are present throughout the round, but you'll be kept busy enough by the course that you won't spend much time standing and staring.

The clubhouse occupies part of a fortified building with construction dating to 1405, and the club claims it as the oldest golf clubhouse in the world. It's a good claim and a good story; it's worth knowing that rival claimants to that title exist, so treat it as a strong piece of golfing folklore rather than settled history.

Visitors are welcome, though weekends are more restricted. A handicap certificate is required, and advance booking is essential. Green fees are in the mid-range for the circuit; for current figures, go directly to ardglassgolfclub.com as pricing varies by season.

## Day Three: Royal County Down

By day three, you're ready for it. Royal County Down in Newcastle was established in 1889, with the Championship Links originally laid out by Old Tom Morris and later refined by Harry Vardon and Harry Colt. The Championship course plays to par 71 at approximately 7,186 yards, with a course rating of 75.0 and a slope of 142. The Annesley Links (par 66, 4,617 yards) is a strong option for a second round or a fourth day.

The setting is the thing you can't quite prepare for: towering marram grass dunes, Dundrum Bay stretching out to one side, the Mourne Mountains filling the skyline behind. It is, by most serious rankings, one of the top courses in the world.

Visitor access runs Monday, Tuesday and Friday all day; Thursday mornings; Sunday afternoons. No access on Wednesdays or Saturdays. Buggies are not permitted — this is a walking course, and caddies are available. All play is in four-ball groups. Advance booking via royalcountydown.org is essential; don't expect to arrange this at short notice.

Green fees on the Championship Links are premium, and the figures quoted across various sources vary enough that it's worth going directly to the club website for current pricing before you budget the trip.

## Getting There

Belfast has two airports: George Best Belfast City (BHD) is typically the more convenient for the County Down circuit, sitting around 30 to 40 minutes from Newcastle by car. Belfast International (BFS) is the larger option with more routes.

If you'd rather take the ferry, Stena Line runs from Cairnryan in Scotland to Belfast (around 2 hours 15 minutes) and from Liverpool Birkenhead to Belfast overnight. P&O runs Cairnryan to Larne in roughly two hours. The ferry crossing, particularly the overnight route from Liverpool, works well if you're bringing your own clubs and want to avoid airline baggage fees.

## Where to Stay

Newcastle is the obvious base. The Slieve Donard Hotel sits adjacent to Royal County Down and is the most famous option; the Burrendale Hotel is a four-star alternative in the same town. Staying in Newcastle keeps you within easy reach of all three courses without needing to move accommodation mid-trip.

## The Takeaway

Three days, three genuinely different links experiences, a range of green fees from modest to premium. Kirkistown gives you quick-draining links terrain at low cost and a proper warm-up. Ardglass gives you clifftop drama and a course that would headline most other counties. Royal County Down gives you one of the best rounds of golf you're likely to play anywhere. That's a proper golf trip, not just a pilgrimage to one famous name.
