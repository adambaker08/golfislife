## The question most buying guides dodge

Electric vs push trolley is one of those decisions the golf industry loves to make complicated. The comparison articles tend to list the same features back and forth without ever telling you what to actually do with the information.

This post tries to do better. For UK walking golfers, the choice comes down to a handful of questions about your own game, your courses, and your car boot. Answer those honestly and the right trolley type usually becomes obvious.

There is no single right answer, but there is almost certainly a right answer for you.

---

## First, ditch the two-wheeled pull trolley

Before anything else: if you are still using an old two-wheeled pull trolley, it is worth retiring it. Pulling a bag on two wheels from one side of your body throughout a round puts repetitive, uneven strain on your back and shoulder. Three-wheeled push trolleys have been the sensible baseline for UK golfers for years. They handle uneven lies, wet rough, and awkward cambers far better. Start there if you are not already.

---

## What actually separates electric from push

The core difference is simple. An electric trolley has a motor and battery. You steer it; it moves itself. A push trolley you push throughout the round.

That sounds obvious, but it has a chain of consequences:

- **Weight.** Electric trolleys are heavier because of the motor and battery. You will notice this lifting the trolley in and out of your car boot more than you will notice it on the course, because you are not pushing the weight around the fairway.
- **Storage.** Push trolleys tend to offer more storage as standard. Electric models can catch up with accessories, but it is worth checking before you buy.
- **Cost.** Push trolleys start significantly lower. The PowaKaddy Micra sits around £189 and the Clicgear 4.0 around £219. Electric trolleys begin at a higher price point and rise steeply from there, with follow and remote models at the premium end costing considerably more.
- **Bag compatibility.** Whichever type you choose, your bag and trolley need to work together as a pair. Worth confirming before committing to either.

## The question that settles it for most UK golfers: what are your courses like?

This is the most useful filter. If you regularly play hilly parkland or links courses with significant elevation change, an electric trolley earns its price difference on the course itself.

Good electric models designed for the UK market include downhill control systems (Motocaddy refers to theirs as DHC) that regulate speed on descents, automatic parking brakes, and anti-tip wheels. On a steep slope with a heavy bag, that is not a gimmick. It makes a material difference to both effort and safety.

Push trolleys on hilly courses mean you are actively fighting the slope on the way down and working hard on the way up. For fit golfers who play occasionally, that is fine. If you play several times a week, it adds up.

On flat or gently undulating courses, the electric advantage shrinks considerably. A good three-wheel push trolley handles those conditions well and costs notably less.

---

## Battery: the detail that actually matters on electrics

If you go electric, the battery specification is not a marketing afterthought. It determines whether the trolley makes it round 18 holes on a cold, wet November morning in Scotland, which is a harder test than a dry July round in Surrey.

Lithium batteries are the right choice over older lead-acid types. They are lighter, they maintain charge better over time with normal care, and they come in 18-hole, 27-hole, and 36-hole configurations. Match the battery to how you typically play and to the conditions you play in. If you regularly play long courses or walk 36 holes in a day, buy more battery capacity than you think you need.

---

## How often do you play?

This matters more than most guides acknowledge.

If you play twice a week or more, the cumulative effort of pushing a trolley over a full season is significant. Electric starts to look like a reasonable investment in energy conservation, especially later in the round when fatigue affects your swing more than you might like to admit.

If you play once a fortnight, the physical effort is not really an issue, and the extra cost of an electric trolley is harder to justify unless the course genuinely demands it.

## Notable brands in the UK market

Three manufacturers dominate what you will encounter at UK pro shops and online:

**PowaKaddy** has been making trolleys in the UK since 1983. Their FX and CT electric ranges are common sights on UK courses, and their Micra is a popular entry point for push trolleys.

**Motocaddy** is the other major electric specialist. The S1 is a solid entry-level option; the M-range steps up in features, and the M7 GPS Remote sits at the premium end with remote control operation.

**Stewart Golf** makes their trolleys in the UK. The R1-S is a push trolley built from aerospace-grade aluminium and priced accordingly at the top of the push market. Their Q Follow electric uses Bluetooth to follow you around the course, which is the closest thing to having a caddy without actually having one, though it sits at the top of the price range.

None of these brands are affiliated partners here, so there are no links, but all three are genuinely worth looking at when you are researching.

## Features worth paying for versus features to skip

**Worth paying for on an electric trolley:** downhill control, automatic parking brake, anti-tip wheels, compact fold (remember, you are lifting this in and out of the boot).

**Probably not worth paying for:** a built-in GPS if you already use a watch or handheld device you are happy with. Remote control if your courses are flat and you are not interested in the novelty.

---

## The honest takeaway

For hilly UK courses and golfers who play regularly, electric makes a strong practical case. For flatter courses, occasional golfers, or those who prefer to keep things simple and affordable, a good three-wheel push trolley does the job without the extra cost or the boot-lifting.

Work out which of those describes your situation and you have largely made the decision already.
