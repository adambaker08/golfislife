There is a familiar pattern to how British golfers plan a links trip. Scotland gets the call first. Ireland second. The Open rota courses if budget allows. And somewhere in the planning, Devon and Cornwall get a polite nod before being quietly dropped in favour of somewhere with a more recognisable name on the scorecard.

That is a mistake worth correcting. The Atlantic-facing coastline from the Somerset border down through North Devon and into Cornwall holds a concentration of genuine, sand-based links golf that would be the envy of most European countries. Fifteen courses from this region sit inside England's top hundred. Six of them have formally grouped together under the banner of the Atlantic Links Golf Coast. The conditions underfoot are playable year-round thanks to the free-draining dune land, and the crowds you'd find at Turnberry or Waterville simply aren't here.

If you haven't been, it's worth understanding what you'd actually be visiting.

## The Courses That Should Anchor Your Itinerary

### St Enodoc (Church Course)

This is the headliner. James Braid's 1907 design near Rock in North Cornwall is consistently described as the finest links in England outside the Open Championship rota, and it makes regular appearances in world top-hundred rankings. It sits across the Camel Estuary from Padstow, tucked into a landscape of dunes and estuary views that doesn't look especially English until you're in the middle of it and very much aware that it is.

Booking well in advance is essential. It is not a course that sits quietly waiting for you to show up.

### Saunton East and West (North Devon)

Saunton is, for many links enthusiasts, the more interesting find. The East Course, designed by Herbert Fowler in the 1920s and set within the Braunton Burrows dune system, features fourteen par fours across its eighteen holes and has long been discussed as a potential future Open venue. The exposed 136-yard thirteenth is a par three that looks manageable on paper and rarely plays that way. Sergio Garcia won the Boys' Amateur here in 1997.

The West Course offers a slightly shorter, less severe alternative, and having two full layouts at the same venue makes Saunton an obvious base for a two-day stop.

### Royal North Devon (Westward Ho!)

Founded in 1864 and still playing over its original fairways, Royal North Devon holds a reasonable claim to being the oldest golf course in England in continuous use on the same ground. It sits on the coast near the town of Westward Ho!, which is either atmospheric or bleak depending on the weather, and frequently both at the same time.

It rewards the golfer who takes a moment to understand what they're playing. The layout, the history, and the setting combine to make it unlike anything else in English golf.

### Burnham and Berrow (Championship Course)

Technically Somerset rather than Devon, Burnham and Berrow sits on the Bristol Channel coast and is regularly used for Open Championship qualifying. That alone tells you something about the standard of the test. The Championship course runs through classic duneland with enough variety across eighteen holes to leave most visiting golfers with a clear sense of what they did wrong and an equal desire to come back and do it again.

### Trevose (Championship Course)

Harry Colt's 1925 design near Trevose Head in Cornwall plays very differently depending on the wind, which on this stretch of coastline can change the character of the course substantially from one morning to the next. Sheltered in part by the headland, it isn't quite as raw as Saunton when conditions turn, but it demands straight thinking. A good option if you're building a North Cornwall itinerary around St Enodoc.

### Perranporth

Described by those who know the region well as probably the most underrated links in the South West, Perranporth is a Harry Colt design that runs over twelve holes along the headland. It lacks the prestige of St Enodoc and the championship pedigree of Saunton, but for golfers who want to find something that isn't already on every list, it's worth seeking out.

## Practical Matters

**Structure your trip in two halves.** The North Devon courses (Saunton, Royal North Devon) cluster near Barnstaple and Braunton. The North Cornwall courses (St Enodoc, Trevose, Perranporth) cluster around Rock, Wadebridge, and the Camel Estuary. A sensible approach is a base in each, allowing you to walk or drive a short distance between venues rather than spending half the trip on the A39.

**Drive yourself.** Public transport to these coastal venues is limited. A self-drive trip is the standard approach and, given how spread out the courses are, essentially the only practical one.

**Regarding green fees.** Published prices from older sources are unreliable, and specific figures haven't been confirmed here, so contact each club directly for current visitor rates. Most operate visitor tee time bookings online.

**Book early, particularly for St Enodoc.** Demand from visitors is consistent, and weekend slots at the top courses can disappear months ahead. Midweek flexibility helps.

**Year-round playability is a genuine advantage.** Because these courses sit on sand-based dune systems, they drain quickly and rarely close for weather conditions that would make an inland parkland course unplayable. An autumn or winter trip is a perfectly reasonable option.

## The Honest Case for Going

Devon and Cornwall golf doesn't have the marketing machinery of the Scottish Highlands or the Kerry coast. It doesn't need it, really. The courses exist, the quality is there, and the lack of hype means visitor numbers at most venues remain manageable. If your default response to "links trip" is to start looking at flights to Edinburgh or Dublin, it's worth pausing to check whether a drive to the South West might deliver something just as good and considerably less expected.
