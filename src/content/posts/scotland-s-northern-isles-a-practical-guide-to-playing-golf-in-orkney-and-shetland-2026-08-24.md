## The Golf Trip Most Travelling Golfers Never Consider

Mention a Scottish golf trip and most people start drawing up itineraries around St Andrews, Carnoustie, or perhaps the Hebrides. Scotland's Northern Isles barely register. That is a shame, because playing golf in Orkney and Shetland is one of the genuinely unusual experiences left in British golf: dramatic coastal settings, courses run on honesty-box systems, wildlife wandering across the fairways, and green fees that are remarkably cheap compared to almost anywhere else in Scotland.

This guide covers the key courses on both island groups and, just as importantly, the practical side of getting there and getting around once you arrive.

---

## Orkney: Older Than You Think, Better Than You Expect

### Orkney Golf Club, Kirkwall

The oldest and longest course in Orkney, founded in 1889, Orkney Golf Club sits on the Mainland just outside Kirkwall. It hosted the golf competition at the 2025 Island Games, which gives you some sense of its standing locally. The club is run as a non-profit by volunteers, which tells you a lot about the atmosphere: expect warmth, informality, and no great fuss.

In midsummer, the club runs what it calls Open Midnight Golf on its fixture card. If you can organise your ferry or flight to land with a few days either side of the solstice, getting out on the course at midnight in reasonable daylight is the sort of thing you will not forget easily.

### Stromness Golf Club

Frequently described as one of the most picturesque courses in the UK, Stromness comes in at just over 4,800 yards. That sounds gentle until the wind arrives off Hoy Sound, at which point the Hoy Hills backdrop becomes less of a view and more of a reminder of what you are up against. Short, but the wind makes it a genuine test.

### South Ronaldsay Golf Club

To reach South Ronaldsay Golf Club at St Margaret's Hope, you drive south from Kirkwall across all four Churchill Barriers — concrete causeways built during the Second World War and now one of Orkney's more striking drives. The course opened in 2005 and offers nine holes with eighteen tees, making it play as a full round in terms of variety. By local accounts it may be the most demanding layout in Orkney.

### Westray Golf Club

This is the one that tends to raise eyebrows. Westray Golf Club sits on the island of Westray, reachable by a short inter-island flight or a ferry from Kirkwall. Its third hole is claimed to be the longest hole in British golf, measuring 738 yards. No booking is required; the green fee operates on an honesty-box system. The course sits next to Noltland Castle and is walking distance from Pierowall village. As a place to spend a morning, it is hard to argue with.

## Shetland: Britain's Most Northerly Golf, and a Fair Bit More

### Shetland Golf Club, Dale

The main 18-hole track on Shetland Mainland sits just north of Lerwick at Dale. It is parkland in character, over 5,500 yards, hilly, and reliably windy. Club hire is available, though payment is currently cash or cheque only, so plan accordingly. A decent base for a couple of rounds if you are spending a few days in Lerwick.

### Whalsay Golf Club

The headline act in Shetland, and by some accounts one of the highest-rated visitor courses anywhere in the UK, Whalsay Golf Club is also Britain's most northerly golf course. Getting there means a 30-minute ferry from Shetland Mainland. The panoramic views are well documented; less expected are the wildlife interruptions — great skuas (locally called bonxies) that will absolutely come for you if you walk near a nest, plus gulls, seals offshore, and occasional porpoises visible from higher holes. The adjacent airstrip occasionally sees charter planes landing mid-round, which adds its own kind of atmosphere.

### Other Options on Shetland

For something more relaxed, Asta Golf Course near Scalloway offers nine holes and is well suited to visitors who want a gentler game. In Lerwick itself, The Knab is a public nine-hole course in the town centre, walkable from the historic waterfront.

For the genuinely committed, Fair Isle has a remote six-hole course established in the early twentieth century, sometimes called the Lighthouse Keeper's Golf Course. Fair Isle is accessible by ferry or small plane from Shetland Mainland and is also one of the better spots in Britain for whale watching, if you are looking for reasons to extend the visit.

Like Orkney, Shetland offers the possibility of late-evening rounds in summer, known locally as the simmer dim. The light in June and July at this latitude is unlike anywhere else in Britain.

## Getting There and Getting Around

**To Orkney:** NorthLink Ferries run from Aberdeen to Kirkwall and from Scrabster to Stromness. Loganair operates flights to Kirkwall from several Scottish airports including Inverness, Edinburgh, and Aberdeen.

**To Shetland:** NorthLink Ferries run overnight from Aberdeen to Lerwick. Loganair flies to Lerwick (Sumburgh Airport) from several Scottish cities.

**Between islands:** Both island groups have their own inter-island ferry networks and Loganair inter-island flights. Westray and Whalsay are both feasible day trips from the respective Mainlands, though you will want to check timetables carefully before building an itinerary around either.

**One important practical note:** clubhouses across these islands are frequently volunteer-run and may not be staffed when you turn up. Several courses operate honesty-box payment. Do not assume a bar, a trolley for hire, or anyone to answer the phone will be available on the day. A bit of advance planning goes a long way.

## Worth the Effort

The Northern Isles will not suit every travelling golfer. The journey takes a bit of organising, the weather can be uncompromising, and you will not find resort infrastructure anywhere near it. What you will find is golf that feels like it belongs to a place, courses where the green fee does not make your eyes water, and the genuine novelty of playing the longest hole in British golf on an island most people could not find on a map. That is not a bad combination.
