Choosing between an electric or push trolley for UK courses sounds straightforward until you start reading the reviews. Most comparisons turn into a features list: motor torque, fold dimensions, GPS integration, wheel diameter. Useful enough, but they rarely answer the question most golfers actually have, which is "which one is right for the courses I play and the way I use it?"

This post cuts through that by focusing on three practical factors that matter more than any spec sheet: how hilly your regular courses are, how much boot space you have, and how often you actually play.

## Factor one: how hilly are your courses?

This is the biggest variable, and it cuts both ways.

On flat to moderately undulating courses, a quality three-wheel push trolley does the job without fuss. You push, it rolls, your bag arrives at the green. The physical effort is real but not punishing, and for many golfers walking a relatively benign parkland layout that effort is part of why they chose to walk in the first place.

On seriously hilly terrain, the calculation shifts. Electric trolleys do the hard work on the climbs, which is the obvious advantage. Less obvious is what happens on steep descents. A heavy bag and trolley gathering momentum on a slope is a genuine problem on many UK heathland and links courses, and it is worth knowing that some electric models include downhill braking systems specifically designed to manage this. The Motocaddy M1 DHC is one example. If your home course has the kind of descents where you have had to sprint after a rogue trolley, that feature moves from nice-to-have to genuinely useful.

For push trolleys on hilly terrain, braking is still a consideration. Look for a solid foot or hand brake rather than relying on the frame alone. It makes a real difference on awkward lies and cross-slope approaches to tees.

## Factor two: boot space and transport

Electric trolleys are heavier and bulkier than push trolleys. The motor, battery, and gearbox add weight and volume that do not disappear when you fold the trolley down. Many electric models fold compactly enough for a standard car boot, but they are still heavier to lift in and out, particularly after eighteen holes.

Push trolleys fold smaller and are easier to handle. Some dismantle into genuinely compact packages, which matters if you share a boot with other gear, travel to courses in a smaller car, or regularly pack for a golf trip.

If boot space is tight or you frequently travel with the trolley, it is worth checking the actual folded dimensions of any electric model you are considering rather than taking "compact fold" in a marketing description at face value. Push trolleys have a consistent edge here.

Storage on the trolley itself goes the other way, with push models generally offering more compartments and accessory space than electric equivalents at a similar price point.

## Factor three: how often do you play?

Frequency of play is probably the most honest lens through which to look at the price difference.

Electric trolleys start at around £499 for entry-level models such as the Motocaddy SE and climb steeply from there. Follow and remote models from brands such as Stewart Golf sit at £1,600 or more. Push trolleys start well below that, with solid options from PowaKaddy and Clicgear available for under £250.

That gap is easier to justify if you are playing two or three times a week across a long season. It is harder to justify for a golfer who gets out fifteen or twenty times a year. Neither is a wrong answer; they are just different situations, and it is worth being honest with yourself about which one applies.

Battery sizing is worth considering for electric buyers specifically. A standard 18-hole lithium battery covers a normal round, but if you regularly play hilly courses, do back-to-back days, or walk a 27-hole layout, a larger battery capacity makes sense. Running out of charge mid-round and pushing a heavy electric trolley the rest of the way is a poor outcome that is straightforward to avoid.

## A quick word on brands

The main players in the UK electric market are Motocaddy, PowaKaddy, Stewart Golf, MGI, and Hill Billy. On the push side, PowaKaddy, Motocaddy, Clicgear, Big Max, and Bag Boy all have solid reputations. All are mentioned here informally; none carry affiliate links.

## The honest takeaway

If you play flat to moderate courses, have a standard or smaller boot, and get out less than once a week on average, a good push trolley will cover everything you need at a fraction of the cost and weight of an electric.

If you play regularly on hilly courses, have no boot space concerns, and would genuinely use the trolley enough to spread the cost, the comfort and effort saving of an electric model is a real benefit, not just a convenience.

The spec sheets matter once you have made that first decision. Start with these three questions and the right category tends to pick itself.
