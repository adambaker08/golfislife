Choosing between an electric vs push golf trolley is one of those decisions that sounds simple until you start reading about it, at which point you're suddenly knee-deep in lithium battery specs, remote-control upgrades, and marketing language designed to make a three-wheel trolley sound like a lunar rover.

Let's cut through that. The right choice depends on a handful of practical variables, and most of the flashy features at the top of the market are genuinely irrelevant to the average club golfer.

## The case for sticking with a push trolley

Push trolleys have improved significantly. The best current options fold down small, weigh very little, and get out of the way when you need them to. Names worth looking at include the Big Max Blade IP 2, with its FlatFold mechanism, the PowaKaddy DLX-Lite FF, Clicgear 4.0, and the Motocaddy Cube and Z1. BagBoy's Nitron also has a loyal following for its auto-open mechanism.

The practical advantages of a push trolley are real:

- **Lower cost.** Entry-level push trolleys cost a fraction of even a basic electric model.
- **No charging logistics.** No battery to forget to charge the night before a Sunday medal.
- **Lighter and simpler to lift.** Into the boot, out of the boot, repeated twice a week for years.
- **More storage as standard.** Push trolleys typically carry more onboard accessories out of the box: scorecard holders, phone pockets, accessory consoles.

If your home course is relatively flat, a push trolley does the job without complication. Many golfers who switch to electric on a flatter parkland course later admit it was more upgrade than they needed.

One thing worth avoiding regardless of format: traditional two-wheel pull trolleys. Pulling a bag on two wheels places uneven strain on one side of the body throughout a round. Three-wheel push trolleys are a better habit, even if you're not ready to go electric.

## When electric starts to make sense

The honest case for electric is terrain and cumulative effort across a season. If your course is hilly or genuinely undulating, the difference between pushing up a slope for eighteen holes and pressing a button is not trivial. It adds up over a full round, and it adds up further over the course of a year.

The two dominant brands in the UK electric market are PowaKaddy (the FX and CT ranges) and Motocaddy (from the entry-level SE up through the M5 GPS and M7 GPS Remote). Stewart Golf is a British-made premium option with a strong reputation for build quality.

A few features worth understanding before you buy:

### Lithium batteries are now the standard, and that matters

Modern electric trolleys use lithium batteries rather than the older lead-acid type. Lithium is lighter, charges faster, and holds up better over time. Most current lithium models are rated for multiple rounds on a single charge, though real-world performance varies depending on terrain and usage.

The key practical point: lithium batteries are easier to live with. Charging overnight before a round is straightforward, and they don't require the maintenance cycles that older battery types did.

### Electronic braking is worth having on hilly courses

Motocaddy's downhill control system (they label it DHC) automatically slows the trolley on descending slopes. PowaKaddy has an equivalent electronic braking system (EBS). If you play a course with steep downhill sections, this is a genuinely useful feature rather than a gimmick. Without it, you're managing the pace of a loaded trolley on a slope by hand, which isn't a disaster but is an extra thing to think about.

### Boot space and fold size: check before you buy

Electric trolleys are heavier than push equivalents because of the motor and battery. That makes a compact, smooth fold more important, not less. Check the folded dimensions of any electric model against the boot of your car before committing. This is one of those things that sounds obvious and regularly catches people out anyway.

### GPS-integrated trolleys

Mid-range electrics like the Motocaddy M5 GPS include a yardage display showing front, middle, and back of green distances and hazard information. For golfers who don't already use a GPS watch or handheld device, this is a reasonable addition. For golfers who already have a GPS watch, it's largely redundant, and not worth paying extra for on that basis alone.

### Follow and remote options

At the top of the market sit follow trolleys (which track your movement autonomously) and remote-controlled models like the Motocaddy M7 Remote and the Stewart Golf Q Follow. These carry a significant price premium over standard electric models and are genuinely useful for golfers with mobility considerations. For most regular club golfers, they're a luxury rather than a necessity.

## How to actually decide

Here is a straightforward way to think about it:

**Choose push if:** your course is fairly flat, you value simplicity, you're budget-conscious, or you want maximum storage and minimum faff.

**Choose electric if:** your course is hilly or long-walking, you play frequently and fatigue across the season is a real factor, or you simply want to enjoy the round without thinking about the trolley.

Reliability and fold size matter more in day-to-day use than GPS screens or remote controls. Most real-world owners rank those two factors above anything else when asked what they'd prioritise if buying again.

The right trolley is the one that suits your course, fits your boot, and doesn't create a new logistical problem in place of the old one.
