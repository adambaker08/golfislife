There is a stretch of the English coastline that most golfers never seriously consider. They head north to Ayrshire, south to Lancashire, or west to Anglesey, and Cumbria sits there on the map, slightly inconvenient, slightly off the radar, getting on with things. That turns out to be precisely its appeal. Playing golf in Cumbria means arriving at courses that feel genuinely unhurried, set against scenery that shifts between the Solway Firth, the Irish Sea, and the Lake District fells depending on which way you turn your head. The green fees are not what you would pay at a marquee name. The car parks are not full of tour buses. And the golf, across at least three clubs on the west coast, is quietly exceptional.

This is not a hidden gem article in the lazy sense. These are old, established, properly serious golf clubs. They are overlooked because they are effortful to reach, not because they are unworthy of the effort.

## Silloth-on-Solway: The Main Event

Silloth-on-Solway Golf Club is the anchor of any Cumbrian golf trip. Founded in 1892 by the North British Railway Company and laid out originally by Davy Grant, it later received input from Willie Park Jr., Willie Fernie, and, in the pre-war years, Dr Alister MacKenzie, whose budget at Silloth was modest enough that only a few of his proposals were ever implemented. That history alone gives the course a certain pedigree.

It sits about 25 miles west of Carlisle on the northern Cumbrian coast, looking across the Solway Firth to the Scottish Borders. Turn around and the Lake District fells fill the eastern skyline. The turf is firm, the fairways undulate in the way that good links fairways should, and heather and gorse make stray drives expensive. It consistently appears in top-50 rankings for Britain and Ireland and has hosted Regional Qualifying for The Open Championship, the British Ladies Championship, the English Amateur Championship, and as recently as 2022, the Carris Trophy.

The course is regularly described as one of the most remote of England's genuinely great layouts. That description is fair. It is also regularly noted as underpriced relative to its quality, though green fees change, so check the club's website directly before planning a trip. Do not rely on any figure you find cited in older articles online.

## Seascale: Links Golf Beside the Irish Sea

About an hour south of Silloth, Seascale Golf Club occupies a strip of the west Cumbrian coast that faces directly out to the Irish Sea. Founded in 1893 and designed by Willie Campbell and George Lowe, it plays to around 6,450 yards with a Standard Scratch of 71 and appears in both the Rolex World's Top 1000 Golf Courses and top-100 England rankings.

On a clear day the Isle of Man is visible to the west and the Lake District peaks frame the eastern horizon. Some holes look north towards the Sellafield plant, which is visible but unremarkable in the way that any industrial structure becomes background detail when you are standing on a links fairway with a westerly in your face. The holes that face the open Irish Sea are the ones that stay with you.

Visitors are welcome, and the club has practice facilities and buggy hire available. For golfers who want a two-day itinerary focused on the north Cumbrian coast, Silloth and Seascale make a natural pairing.

## Furness Golf Club: The One on Walney Island

Furness Golf Club, known as the Old Links, sits on Walney Island just across the Jubilee Bridge from Barrow-in-Furness and carries a founding date of 1872, making it the third oldest links course and sixth oldest golf club in England. It started as a six-hole course on Biggar Bank, created by Scottish migrant workers from Dundee, and has grown to a full 18 holes playing to 6,363 yards at par 71.

Every hole has views of either the Irish Sea or the Lakeland hills. The par-3 tenth drops roughly 100 feet from tee to pin, which is the kind of hole that gets remembered. The club describes itself as probably the best-kept golfing secret in the north-west, and while that sort of self-description usually invites scepticism, the history and setting genuinely earn the claim here.

Visitors are welcome every day of the week.

## Getting There and Making a Week of It

The honest answer on logistics is that a car makes this trip significantly more practical than public transport if you are combining all three clubs. Silloth is not on a main rail line; Carlisle is the nearest hub. Seascale is served by the Cumbrian Coast line running between Carlisle and Barrow, which is a scenic and useful option if you are happy to plan around train times. Walney Island requires Barrow-in-Furness as a base.

A self-drive itinerary following the Cumbrian coastal route threads these clubs together neatly. Carlisle Golf Club (parkland, founded 1908, just off junction 43 of the M6) and Penrith Golf Club provide decent options for non-links days or for groups where not everyone wants links golf every day of the trip. Accommodation ranges from B&Bs in Silloth itself to larger hotels near Carlisle.

## Why Bother?

Because the golf is genuinely good, the crowds are genuinely absent, and the landscape is not quite like anywhere else in England. Cumbria's west coast links exist in a particular kind of quiet that courses closer to motorways and metropolitan areas have long since lost. If you are the sort of golfer who values that, and who does not mind a longer drive or an awkward rail connection as the price of admission, this corner of England will repay the effort considerably.

Check current visitor green fees directly with each club before booking. Prices vary by season and day, and anything quoted elsewhere may be out of date.
