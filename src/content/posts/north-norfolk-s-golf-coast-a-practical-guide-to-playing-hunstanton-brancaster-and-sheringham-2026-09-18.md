England has no shortage of celebrated links stretches, but the north Norfolk coast earns far less attention than it deserves. Within a corridor of roughly 25 miles, three courses of genuine quality sit close enough to combine into a two or three-day trip: Royal West Norfolk at Brancaster, Hunstanton, and Sheringham. The problem is that most trip-planning articles either treat them in isolation or gloss over the practical wrinkles that can derail a visit before you've hit a shot. This post treats the three as a coherent circuit and deals with those wrinkles honestly.

## Why North Norfolk Works as a Golf Trip

The practical case starts with the weather. Norfolk has the sunniest and driest climate in England, which matters considerably when you're comparing it with the west-coast links that tend to dominate bucket lists. A links round on a firm, fast-running course in reasonable sunshine is a different proposition from battling horizontal rain on the Ayrshire coast. That's not a guarantee, but the odds are genuinely better here.

The three courses are close: Hunstanton to Brancaster is under ten miles along the A149, and Sheringham sits roughly 25 miles east. A hire car is essential — there's no rail link to Brancaster or Hunstanton, and while you can reach Kings Lynn by train from London or the Midlands and hire from there, public transport won't get you around the circuit comfortably. Factor that into your planning from the start.

## Royal West Norfolk (Brancaster): The Access Quirks You Need to Know

Royal West Norfolk is the centrepiece of any Norfolk golf trip, and rightly so. Founded in 1892 and laid out by Holcombe Ingleby, the course stretches to 6,457 yards and plays to par 71 between Brancaster Bay and the salt marshes. Very little has changed from the original design, which accounts for much of its character. It's ranked among the top 20 courses in England and regularly appears in world top-100 lists.

Here's what the glossy write-ups tend to leave out.

**The tide issue is real.** The clubhouse sits on ground that becomes cut off at high tide. If you turn up at the wrong point in the tidal cycle, you cannot reach it. Beyond that, the 8th and 9th holes are themselves affected by tidal conditions. The club advises visitors to check tide times before travelling, and they publish relevant information on their website. This is not a minor footnote — plan your tee time around the tides, not the other way around. The UK Hydrographic Office and BBC Weather both carry tide tables for the area.

**The format is foursomes and singles only.** Royal West Norfolk does not permit three- or four-ball play. If you arrive expecting to walk on as a group of four, you won't be playing. Singles or twosomes only, which gives the course a particular atmosphere but requires adjusting your travel group accordingly.

**No weekend reservations, and none during August.** Visitor bookings work differently here than at most clubs. There are no visitor reservations at weekends or throughout August. For green fee rates, you need to contact the Secretary directly — these are not published in a standard tariff format, and making up a figure would be doing you a disservice.

## Hunstanton Golf Club: A Connoisseur's Test

Hunstanton sits at the northern end of the circuit and is perhaps the most visitor-friendly of the three in terms of access. The course was designed by James Braid and opened in 1891, running to 6,741 yards at par 72. The layout follows an out-and-back pattern on either side of a central dune ridge, which keeps things interesting as the wind direction shifts through the day.

It has hosted serious amateur events including the Brabazon Trophy and the British Amateur Championship, which gives some indication of the standard of the examination. Visitors are welcome, and the club offers a 36-hole day option for those who want to make a full day of it. There is a supplementary fee for the second round, though the exact figures should be confirmed directly with the club before your visit as prices can change seasonally.

The course demands creativity and course management rather than simply length. If you play well at Hunstanton, it tends to mean something.

## Sheringham Golf Club: Clifftop Links Worth the Detour

Sheringham is the easiest course to overlook because it sits slightly apart from the Brancaster-Hunstanton pairing. That would be a mistake. Originally laid out by Tom Dunn in 1891 and extended to 18 holes in 1898, the par-70 clifftop course sits above the North Sea with the North Norfolk Railway running alongside the property. It has hosted English Women's Championship events and offers a completely different visual setting from the marshland atmosphere at Brancaster.

The clifftop exposure means wind is a constant factor. At Sheringham, club selection from the tee is rarely straightforward, and the views make for a good reason to take your time between shots.

## Rounding Out the Trip

If you have a fourth day and want to push the circuit further, Royal Cromer sits not far east of Sheringham. It's one of England's Royal clubs, shaped over the years by Old Tom Morris, J.H. Taylor, and James Braid, and offers a similar clifftop character to Sheringham. A detour for completeness rather than a necessity, but worth knowing it's there.

Great Yarmouth and Caister Golf Club is worth mentioning as a curiosity rather than a must-play: founded in 1882, it incorporates the Great Yarmouth racecourse as an active hazard on the course, which makes it genuinely unusual if unusual is what you're after on a rest day.

## The Honest Summary

The north Norfolk coast rewards those who plan carefully and approach it on its own terms. Brancaster in particular has requirements that will catch out anyone who books a tee time without reading the small print. Sort your tides, accept the foursomes format, and contact the Secretary well in advance. Do that, and you have a realistic shot at one of the most distinctive links experiences in English golf, without getting on a plane.
