If you search for trolley buying advice online, you will mostly find ranked lists with a budget pick, a mid-range pick, and a premium pick, and a conclusion that basically tells you electric is always the better option if you can stretch to it. That is not especially useful if you are trying to work out what actually suits your situation.

This post takes a different approach. Instead of ranking products, it works through the decisions that should drive your choice: the terrain you play, how much boot space you have, how often you walk, and what genuinely matters versus what is a nice-to-have extra.

## Start with your course, not your budget

The single most useful question is where you actually play. UK golf throws up an enormous range of terrain, and it matters more for this decision than almost anything else.

If your home course is a relatively flat parkland track, a quality push trolley will do everything you need. You will spend less, get a lighter piece of kit that folds smaller, and not miss much.

If your course has significant hills, either proper elevation change or the slow grinding undulation you find on a lot of links and heathland tracks, an electric trolley earns its keep. The practical advantages are not just about saving your legs on the climb. On downhill sections, a good electric trolley with braking control keeps the bag moving at a steady pace rather than rolling into the back of your heels or picking up speed on a slope. Motocaddy calls this feature DHC, PowaKaddy calls theirs EBS. On genuinely hilly ground, it is not a gimmick.

## The honest case for push trolleys

Push trolleys have a few real advantages that tend to get glossed over in buying guides that are quietly tilting towards more expensive products.

**They are significantly lighter and more compact.** Most fold down into a footprint that fits easily in a smaller car boot, which is a genuine consideration if you share a car, own a hatchback with limited cargo space, or store your gear in a small garage.

**They tend to include more storage as standard.** Many electric models come fairly bare and charge extra for accessory packs to add a phone pocket, drinks holder, or ball tray. A lot of push trolleys include all of that from the box.

**There is nothing to charge.** This sounds trivial until you have forgotten to plug in your electric trolley the night before a 7am tee time. It happens, and it is annoying.

**They are meaningfully cheaper.** You can get a well-made, durable push trolley from brands with solid UK reputations for considerably less than entry-level electric models, which start from roughly £300 to £450 for a lithium-battery version. That gap is worth thinking about honestly if you play once or twice a month.

Models that come up regularly in this category include the Motocaddy Cube Push, the PowaKaddy Micra, the Clicgear 4.0, the PowaKaddy DLX-Lite FF, and the Stewart R1-S, which is manufactured in the UK.

## When electric genuinely makes sense

On hilly courses, electric is the more practical choice for most regular walkers. The motor does the work on inclines, the braking handles descents, and you are not fighting the bag on awkward sidehill lies. Over a four-hour round on undulating ground, that matters.

The sub-type question then becomes relevant. The vast majority of electric trolley buyers in the UK opt for a manual-steer model, where you walk alongside and steer by hand. Remote-control versions let you send the trolley ahead while you walk to your ball. Follow (autonomous) models track you without any input. Both of the latter categories carry a significant price premium, with premium remote and follow models pushing well above the mid-range bracket.

For most regular golfers who walk but are not touring professionals, manual-steer electric covers the ground comfortably. The features worth prioritising are:

- **Lithium battery** rather than older lead-acid. Lighter, better range, longer lifespan.
- **Slope braking control** (whatever your chosen brand calls it) if you play on anything other than flat ground.
- **A reliable one-step fold.** This sounds basic but varies considerably in practice.
- **GPS built in?** Useful if you do not already own a separate device. If you already carry a GPS unit or use a phone app, it is an added cost you may not need.

Mid-range electric models, typically priced in the £450 to £650 range, tend to combine lithium battery, slope braking, and GPS. Names that recur in UK 2026 guides include the Motocaddy M5 GPS DHC, the PowaKaddy CT6, and at the higher end, the Motocaddy M7 GPS Remote.

## A practical checklist before you decide

Before defaulting to "electric is always better," run through these:

1. **Course terrain.** Hilly or flat? Be honest. "A few slopes" is different from 100 metres of elevation change per round.
2. **Boot space.** Measure if you are not sure. Electric trolleys fold down but stay bulkier and heavier than push equivalents.
3. **How often you play.** If you walk 40-plus rounds a year, the investment in electric is easier to justify than if you manage 15.
4. **Storage at home.** A garage is fine for either. A third-floor flat is a different calculation.
5. **Do you already own GPS?** If yes, skip the built-in GPS models unless you want the consolidation.

## The takeaway

Electric is not always the right answer, and push is not just the budget compromise. They suit different golfers on different courses. If you play undulating UK terrain regularly and walk most of your rounds, an electric trolley with a lithium battery and braking control will make the round noticeably easier. If your course is relatively flat and your boot space is limited, a quality push trolley is a sensible, practical choice and not a step down. Work through the checklist above before you let anyone's ranked list make the decision for you.
