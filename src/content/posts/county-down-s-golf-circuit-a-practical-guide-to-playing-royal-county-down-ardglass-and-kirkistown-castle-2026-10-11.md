County Down is one of those corners of the British Isles where a golf trip can feel genuinely disproportionate to the effort involved in getting there. The county is anchored by Royal County Down, a course that sits near the top of almost every serious world ranking, but the surrounding circuit of courses makes a multi-day visit worth proper planning. Ardglass, Kirkistown Castle, and Clandeboye are all within reasonable reach, all welcome visitors, and together they fill out an itinerary that justifies the ferry crossing.

This is a practical guide, not a love letter. Here is how to get there, what each course demands, and how to put the days together sensibly.

## Getting to County Down from the Mainland

The most straightforward route from mainland Britain is via ferry to Belfast. Stena Line and P&O both operate Cairnryan (Stranraer area, southwest Scotland) to Belfast, with a crossing time of around two hours fifteen minutes. If you are coming from further south, there is also an overnight ferry from Liverpool (Birkenhead) to Belfast, which is a reasonable way to avoid a long drive and arrive rested.

Flying into Belfast International or George Best Belfast City Airport is the other option and suits anyone who would rather hire a car on arrival.

From Belfast, Royal County Down at Newcastle is roughly 30 to 45 minutes south on the A24. Ardglass is a further 30 minutes or so along the coast. Kirkistown Castle, on the Ards Peninsula, is around an hour north of Newcastle. You can realistically base yourself in one place for the whole trip. The Slieve Donard Resort and Spa sits immediately next to Royal County Down and is the best-known option for golf groups in the area, though it is worth checking availability and rates directly.

## Royal County Down: What You Actually Need to Know

Royal County Down was laid out originally by Old Tom Morris in 1889 and refined later by Harry Colt. The Championship Links plays to par 71 at just over 7,100 yards, with a slope rating of 142. That number matters. This is a genuinely difficult course, particularly on a first visit, and a caddie is widely recommended. There are multiple blind tee shots, and local knowledge makes a significant difference.

A handicap certificate, or equivalent proof of handicap, is required to play the Championship Links. Make sure you have one before you travel.

Green fees for the Championship Links are at the premium end of the market and have risen in recent years. Rather than quote a figure that may already be out of date, check current rates directly at royalcountydown.org before you book. The Annesley Links (par 66, 4,548 yards) is the club's second course and is considerably more affordable, a useful option if budget is a factor or if someone in your group wants a gentler round.

Visitor days are Monday, Tuesday, and Friday all day; Thursday mornings; and Sunday afternoons. Wednesdays and Saturdays are closed to visitors. Availability is limited, so book well in advance, especially for peak season.

The setting is exceptional. The course sits between the Mourne Mountains and the Irish Sea, within the Murlough Nature Reserve, and the ninth hole in particular has the kind of view that appears on every list of memorable golf holes.

## Ardglass: Clifftop Links with an Unusual History

Ardglass Golf Club is about 30 minutes from Newcastle along the coast, and it is the kind of course that tends to make people wish they had known about it sooner. Founded in 1896, it occupies clifftop ground above Ardglass village with sea views from every hole. The Irish Sea comes into play on several of them.

What makes Ardglass genuinely distinctive is the clubhouse, which occupies a fortified building dating to around 1405. It is widely cited as one of the oldest buildings in the world used as a golf clubhouse, which is a peculiar claim to fame but a real one.

At par 70 and approximately 6,268 yards from the medal tees, this is not a course that will flatten you on paper. But the clifftop exposure and the terrain keep it interesting. Visitors are welcome, and it pairs naturally with Royal County Down as part of a County Down golf circuit day.

## Kirkistown Castle: The Accessible Links

Kirkistown Castle Golf Club sits on the Ards Peninsula at Cloughey, roughly an hour north of Newcastle. It was redesigned by James Braid in 1934 and is built on a sandy base that keeps it playable year-round. It is often described as the closest true links course to Belfast.

Green fees are significantly cheaper than Royal County Down, and the club welcomes visitors seven days a week, which gives it flexibility the other courses on the circuit do not always have. If you want to add a third or fourth round to a trip, Kirkistown Castle is the obvious candidate.

## Clandeboye: Worth Knowing About

Near Bangor on the north Down coast, Clandeboye has two courses. The Dufferin is a heathland and parkland layout in the six-thousand-yard range; the Ava is the shorter option. Both welcome visitors primarily on weekdays. The midweek fees are in a similar range to Kirkistown Castle, though it is worth confirming current rates with the club directly. It is not a links, but if you want variety across a longer trip, it rounds out the options.

## Putting the Trip Together

A workable structure for four days might look like this: arrive via ferry, play Kirkistown Castle or Clandeboye on the first afternoon or morning, spend two days around Newcastle with Royal County Down as the centrepiece (and the Annesley Links as a shorter option), and use Ardglass as a final round before the journey back.

The honest takeaway is this: Royal County Down deserves its reputation, and it should be the main event. But County Down's golf circuit around it is better than most visitors realise, and it is genuinely easy to build a trip where every round feels like a proper reason to be there.
