Walking 18 holes in the UK covers somewhere between five and six miles, frequently over undulating, wet, and occasionally brutal terrain. A trolley exists to manage that physical cost so you arrive at the 18th with something left in the tank. But between a £60 three-wheeler and a £1,400 remote-control GPS electric model, the choice isn't obvious. This post works through how to actually choose the right one for your situation, rather than just listing specs from a brochure.

## Start with your course, not the product page

Before you look at a single model, think about where you play most of your golf.

A flat, well-maintained parkland course with firm paths is a fundamentally different environment from a heaving links or a steep inland course with switchback fairways. On forgiving terrain, a decent push trolley is entirely sufficient. On a course with sharp descents, a powered electric trolley with downhill braking becomes a more serious consideration, and not merely a luxury. Motocaddy calls their version DHC; others label it EBS. It prevents the trolley running away from you on steep drops, which is a real problem on certain UK layouts and not a gimmick.

If you play a variety of courses, base your decision on the worst terrain you regularly encounter, not the easiest.

## The honest case for push trolleys

Push (manual) trolleys are lighter, need no charging, require minimal maintenance, and cost considerably less. If you want a lightweight aluminium benchmark, the PowaKaddy TwinLine 5 is often cited at around 7 kg, which is manageable to load and carry between car and first tee.

The practical advantages are straightforward. You do not need to remember to charge anything. There is no battery range anxiety on a long day when the round goes to 20 holes or conditions are harder than expected. Compact folding models fit more easily into smaller car boots, which matters for a meaningful number of UK golfers.

The case against is simple too: you are still expending energy steering and pushing the thing up every hill. If your back, shoulders, or knees make that an issue, a push trolley is not really the cost-effective option it appears.

## The honest case for electric trolleys

Electric trolleys are heavier, cost more, and require more upkeep. They are also genuinely easier to use over 18 holes, because the physical effort of managing the trolley on gradients is removed almost entirely. For golfers who find the pushing aspect physically tiring, that is not a small thing.

Current lithium battery technology on mainstream models is typically rated for 18 to 36 holes per charge. Heavier bags and hilly terrain push you towards the lower end of that range, so do not assume 36 holes is achievable in all conditions.

The mainstream UK electric trolley market runs from roughly £330 at entry level to around £1,500 for remote-control and GPS-integrated models, with a large cluster of well-regarded options in the £500 to £800 range. Most club golfers are well served somewhere in that middle band. The leading UK brands are Motocaddy and PowaKaddy, who between them cover the majority of the market. Stewart Golf, Big Max, Sun Mountain, and Wishbone each have their followers too.

## Two features worth thinking carefully about

**Remote control.** Remote models roughly double the price of their equivalent push-button counterparts. They allow you to send the trolley ahead while you play the shot, which is useful on very hilly courses and practical for golfers with back or shoulder problems. For everyone else, it is a convenience rather than a necessity. Decide whether that gap in price is justified by your specific situation, not by the demo video.

**Built-in GPS.** If you already use a GPS watch or a phone app for yardages, a trolley with integrated GPS is largely duplicating something you have already paid for and adds several hundred pounds to the price. The trolley-GPS post-on-top-of-existing-GPS question is genuinely one of the most common pieces of overbuying in this category. The narrow exception is if you want your yardages displayed on the trolley handlebars and prefer not to glance at your wrist.

## The boot space question (do not skip this one)

This is underrated as a buying factor. Many UK golfers drive cars with modest boot space, and a full-size electric trolley, folded, is a substantial object alongside a bag, shoes, and wet-weather gear. Compact folding electric designs exist, but they typically sacrifice accessory storage to achieve the smaller footprint. Check the folded dimensions of any model you are seriously considering before you buy, not afterwards.

## A simple decision framework

- Flat or moderate terrain, no significant joint issues, limited budget: a quality push trolley is the sensible choice.
- Undulating or hilly terrain, or any physical reason to minimise effort: electric makes sense, and look for downhill braking as a feature.
- Already own a GPS device: skip integrated GPS on the trolley.
- Small car boot: check folded dimensions before anything else.
- Remote control: only worth the premium if the terrain or a physical issue genuinely justifies it.

The gap between adjacent models in any electric trolley range often comes down to a single feature. If you do not need that feature, you are paying purely for something that will sit unused. Work backwards from your actual conditions rather than forward from the spec sheet.
